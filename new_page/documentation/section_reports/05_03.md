# 5.3 Theoretical Implications

## Section Purpose

This section discusses the meaning of the empirical findings and compares the proposed system with existing ESG analysis methods. It draws on literature concerning corporate disclosure, sustainability reporting, ESG data analytics, ABSA, greenwashing detection, materiality, and climate disclosure analysis [R003], [R028], [R031], [R032], [R033].

## Discussion

The main empirical finding is that commitment-oriented ESG disclosure dominates the current extracted dataset. Out of 332 records, 115 are classified as `commitment`, while 50 are classified as `outcome`. This imbalance matters because commitment language often signals strategic intention, legitimacy, or aspiration, whereas outcome language provides stronger evidence of realized performance.

The ClimateBERT-style comparison provides a partial validation signal. Commitment tone co-occurs with `climate-commitment` 91 times, with `climate-d` 57 times, and with `environmental-claims` 48 times. This pattern suggests that the extraction layer is not randomly assigning commitment labels; many commitment records also carry climate-related semantic evidence.

However, the same evidence exposes limitations. Missing tones co-occur with `climate-commitment` 22 times and `environmental-claims` 21 times. These cases are likely false negatives or schema failures because climate-relevant claims should usually have an interpretable disclosure tone. Governance disclosures also spread across action, commitment, outcome, none, and missing categories, which indicates that governance language is less easily captured by environmental-centric tone cues.

## Comparison With Existing Methods

Compared with manual ESG scoring, this pipeline is more scalable and produces traceable text-level evidence, but it lacks expert judgment unless annotation is added. Compared with pure LLM classification, the pipeline is more auditable because it stores raw outputs, parsed JSON, prompt metadata, visualizations, and ClimateBERT comparison labels. Compared with ClimateBERT-only approaches, the pipeline is broader because it includes social and governance records and explicitly models disclosure tone. Compared with rule-based taxonomies, the pipeline is more flexible for bilingual and context-dependent language, while still preserving interpretability through lexicons and ontology paths.

## Theoretical Implications

The findings are consistent with legitimacy-oriented interpretations of sustainability reporting: firms may use ESG disclosure to signal responsibility and future alignment even when measured outcomes are less frequent. The low number of social records also raises a stakeholder-theory question about whether selected report pages, extraction prompts, or corporate disclosure practices emphasize environmental and governance audiences more strongly than social stakeholders.

For ABSA theory, the results show why sentiment alone is insufficient. A neutral statement can still be an important commitment or outcome. For greenwashing research, the commitment/outcome imbalance suggests a measurable warning signal, but it must be validated before being treated as a definitive greenwashing index.

## Limitations Discussed

The most important limitation is the absence of full expert-labeled ground truth. The ClimateBERT remote sample has only three inputs, and ClimateBERT is not a direct tone classifier. OCR quality has not been measured with character error rate or table accuracy. Prompt sensitivity remains a concern because seven prompt templates are used. Finally, the ontology may not fully cover Indonesian-specific sustainability disclosure language.

## Implication for the Thesis

The discussion should position the project as an exploratory but reproducible research framework. Its main contribution is not that it has already solved ESG classification, but that it creates the infrastructure for formal benchmark construction and richer ESG disclosure interpretation.

## Section-Specific Report

From a legitimacy-theory perspective, the dominance of commitment language is consistent with companies using ESG reports to signal responsibility and future alignment. From a stakeholder-theory perspective, the pillar distribution (`E`=179, `G`=121, `S`=4) suggests that environmental and governance audiences receive more visible attention in the extracted records than social stakeholders.

For ABSA theory, the results show that sentiment polarity is not enough. A neutral sentence may still be an important commitment or outcome. For greenwashing literature, the commitment/outcome imbalance provides a measurable risk signal, but it should remain a heuristic until validated against expert labels or known cases.
