Background

1) Document heterogeneity and layout issues in machine-readable PDFs/HTML
ESG and climate disclosures arrive in highly heterogeneous formats that challenge machine readability even when text is extractable. PDFs, HTML pages, and slide-like reports exhibit irregular reading orders, multi-column layouts, and nonstandard hierarchies that complicate reliable text segmentation and alignment of content with semantic units such as sections, tables, and figures. Empirical work on ESG parsing repeatedly highlights layout-driven variability that undermines naive extraction pipelines and calls for layout-aware approaches that preserve structure and provenance across issuers, markets, and reporting frameworks (Chen, 2025; Mishra et al., 2024; (Chen, 2025; Cui, 2025). The heterogeneity is not limited to format; even within ostensibly standardized reports, material content appears across diverse sections and modalities, requiring robust handling of reading order, captions, and embedded visual elements to maintain semantic fidelity (Chen, 2025; Mishra et al., 2024; (Chen, 2025; . In addition, multilingual disclosures introduce cross-language variation in terminology and presentation, further complicating cross-document comparability and requiring design choices that accommodate multilingual processing without sacrificing structural signals (Chen, 2025; Hang, 2025; Wang, 2025).

2) Why tables, footnotes, and appendices complicate extraction
Tables encode key quantitative disclosures (emissions, intensity, targets) but their structures are frequently irregular: merged cells, multi-row/column headers, spanning footnotes, and embedded footnotes that qualify or contextualize figures. Appendices and boilerplate disclosures often host critical methodological details (scopes, baselines, calculation methods) that are essential for interpretation but are easy to overlook with token-limited or document-wide extractions. This complexity is well documented in recent ESG/financial-document parsing work, which shows that table structure recognition and table-to-semantic-entity conversion remain nontrivial, especially when documents mix narrative text with dense tabular data and multilingual content (Chen, 2025; Cui, 2025)Lauble, 2025). Layout-aware table reconstruction and provenance tracing are therefore necessary to avoid data losses or misattributions and to enable reliable cross-document synthesis Mishra et al., 2024; (Chen, 2025; Cui, 2025). Footnotes frequently carry constraints, time horizons, or exceptions that alter reported values, making cross-year comparisons sensitive to footnote scope and referencing, thereby demanding explicit linking between tabular data, narrative statements, and their footnotes Mishra et al., 2024; (Chen, 2025; Cui, 2025).

3) High-context nature of ESG/climate language (targets vs. performance, boundaries, uncertainty)
ESG and climate language conveys nuanced, high-context meaning. The same numeric value can reflect different scopes (e.g., Scope 1 vs. Scope 1+2 vs. Scope 3), baselines, and time horizons, with boundaries and methodological choices (e.g., GHG Protocol vs. alternative standards) shaping interpretation. Narrative statements often encode performance signals alongside commitments, risk disclosures, or strategic framing, making it essential to disambiguate whether a sentence denotes an actual achievement, a stated target, or an aspirational plan. Uncertainty, adjustment mechanisms, and regulatory or jurisdictional variation further complicate extraction and comparability across firms and years. The literature emphasizes that the semantic distinction between targets and actual performance, as well as the governance and boundary definitions underpinning reported metrics, must be explicitly captured to support reliable benchmarking and decision-making (Chen, 2025; Kaoukis, 2025; Hang, 2025; Coen et al., 2022). This high-context nature also implies that simple keyword extraction is insufficient; robust analytics require linkage between explicit disclosures, their corroborating evidence (e.g., tables, prior-year data, external standards), and the provenance of each assertion to support auditability Mishra et al., 2024; (Chen, 2025; Cui, 2025).

4) Implications for dataset construction and auditability
Dataset construction for ESG/climate analytics must account for structural diversity, multilingual content, and context-rich semantics. Effective datasets require:

Structure-aware annotation: mapping each data point to its exact source region (section, table row, footnote) and capturing reading order and table context to preserve meaning during downstream modeling (Chen, 2025; Cui, 2025).
Provenance and lineage: explicit traces from extracted facts to source passages, with versioning to enable auditing, change detection, and regulatory review (Ni et al., 2023; Hang, 2025; Mishra et al., 2024; (Chen, 2025; .
Handling of boundaries and harmonization rules: standardized representations for scopes, baselines, and time horizons, while preserving the original boundary definitions to avoid misinterpretation across jurisdictions (Chen, 2025; Wei, 2025; Wang, 2025).
Multilingual coverage: aligned multilingual mappings to enable cross-country comparisons while respecting language-specific terminology and standards (Chen, 2025; Hang, 2025; Wang, 2025).
Reliability-aware evaluation: datasets should support evaluation of extraction accuracy, provenance fidelity, and cross-document consistency under real-world document heterogeneity, including explicit reporting of confidence and failure modes (Li, 2025; Wang, 2025; Lauble, 2025).

Disclosure element	Typical presentation	Common pitfalls
Emissions total (Scope 1)	Narrative sentence, or table row; may vary by year	Misidentification of scope boundary; missing baselines
Emissions total (Scope 2)	Table or narrative; often with location-based or market-based distinctions	Confusion of Scope 1/2/3; missing method references
Emissions intensity (per revenue), metric	Tabular or embedded in KPI lists; unit varies (tCO2e/$ revenue)	Inconsistent units; unlabeled denominators
Net-zero/goals and target year	Narrative commitments; sometimes in dashboards	Ambiguity about baselines; no explicit target date or interim milestones
Baseline year	Often in a footnote or table header	Baseline year missing or misread; year formats vary by jurisdiction
Reporting period	Annual, fiscal-year alignment; sometimes multi-year	Misalignment with 'year ended' dates; offset years confusing
Methodology reference	Footnotes or dedicated section; GHG protocol, standards cited	Vague or absent methodology; no link to external standard
Scope 3 coverage	Narrative or table; may specify categories	Omitted scope 3; incomplete or inconsistent categorization
Verification/audit status	Internal/external audit statements; certificate logos	Ambiguity about verification scope or year; outdated claims
Data provenance/evidence	Citations to passages or tables; page references	Missing provenance; uncertain link between value and source
Confidence/uncertainty statements	Qualitative hedges (may, could, estimates)	Overlooking hedges; treating estimates as certainties
Year-over-year trend	Inline text or chart captions	Misreading trend direction; inconsistent baselines
Regional or jurisdictional scope	Explicit country/region scope; can be partial	Global vs. regional reporting gaps; inconsistent units
Greenhouse gas removal or offset details	Narratives or footnotes; credits cited	Misinterpreting offsets as reductions; double counting
Energy efficiency or intensity metrics	KPI tables or dashboards	Lack of normalization; unclear denominators or activity drivers
Running example (used throughout): A company states a target to reduce Scope 1+2 emissions by 40% by 2030, baseline year 2023, reported as 1.5 million tCO2e in 2022; a footnote clarifies the scope boundary and method. In the background and methods sections, this target is linked to prior-year disclosures and the GHG Protocol methodology. The dataset construction would record the target sentence, associate it with the corresponding table row (baseline 2023, 2022 value as context), capture the footnote for boundary notes, and store provenance entries (page/section, figure caption). This example illustrates the need for explicit provenance, boundary definitions, and cross-referencing to ensure auditability and reproducibility of the extraction.
