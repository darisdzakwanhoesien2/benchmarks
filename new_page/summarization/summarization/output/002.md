Section 3. A Taxonomy of Summarization Methods

This section categorizes summarization methods and explains their relevance to Indonesian ESG disclosures, with a focus on pillar-structured outputs, evidence traceability, and OCR/noise considerations. Each subsection links method characteristics to ESG-centric needs such as factual fidelity, configurability, and governance-auditable narratives.

3.1 Heuristic Extractive Baselines

Definition and core idea

Heuristic extractive baselines rely on simple, interpretable signals to select content directly from the source. Common heuristics include lead-based selection (top sentences or sections), sentence-level salience via frequency (e.g., TF-idf, word or term frequencies), and surface cues like section headers. These approaches do not generate new text and thus preserve exact wording and figures, which supports auditable traceability in ESG contexts (Qi et al., 2022; , Goot et al., 2021), (Sabry et al., 2022; .
ESG examples and relevance

Lead-based extraction aligns with pillar-structured ESG summaries by preserving the opening governance or risk statements that frequently anchor the annual report’s material issues. Frequency-based methods can surface recurring KPI mentions (e.g., emissions intensity, energy use, GHG reduction targets) that appear across sections, helping to produce compact KPI-focused briefs while preserving original metric values for auditability (Qi et al., 2022; , Goot et al., 2021), (Sabry et al., 2022; .
Strengths: high factual fidelity to the source, transparent traceability, robust to OCR noise when the extracted sentences remain unchanged, and easy to audit since quotes are verbatim. Limits: lack of discourse-level coherence, potential omission of cross-cutting evidence, sensitivity to layout and formatting remnants introduced by OCR, and weak handling of multilingual or mixed-content documents where important claims are scattered across sections (Qi et al., 2022; , Goot et al., 2021), (Sabry et al., 2022; .
ESG-specific design notes

In Indonesian sustainability reporting, extractive baselines can reliably capture exact KPI figures, policy citations, and regulator references when these appear in tables or highlighted passages. However, they may fail to connect related claims across sections (e.g., linking a governance policy to its measurable outcome) without additional linking anchors or post-processing (Fei et al., 2022; , Maitama et al., 2020), Alshaikh et al., 2024).
3.2 Graph-Based Extractive Ranking (TextRank Family)

Definition and mechanism

Graph-based extractive ranking constructs a graph where units (sentences or paragraphs) are nodes and edges reflect similarity or co-occurrence. TextRank and its successors rank nodes by importance using a PageRank-inspired algorithm, selecting top-ranked units to form a summary. This approach remains extractive but introduces a principled selection mechanism beyond simple lead or frequency heuristics (Sabry et al., 2022; .
Why it’s used in ESG contexts

TextRank promotes coverage and redundancy reduction while preserving traceable evidence passages, which is valuable for ESG where multiple documents may discuss related claims. The graph-based ranking can surface diverse but salient evidence (e.g., governance claims, environmental KPI updates, stakeholder engagement notes) while maintaining source passages for auditability (Sabry et al., 2022; , (Qi et al., 2022; .
ESG-specific design notes

Strength: better coverage of diverse ESG facets than pure lead or frequency methods; maintain citation integrity since selected units are verbatim. Limitation: still lacks synthesis or paraphrase; may propagate OCR-induced errors and misinterpretations if noisy sentences are selected; may require post-processing to ensure pillar alignment and cross-document coherence (Fei et al., 2022; , Maitama et al., 2020).
3.3 Classical Supervised Summarization

Definition and scope

Classical supervised extractive summarization models train with labeled data to rank or select sentences. Early pipelines used supervised ranking objectives or classifiers to determine sentence importance based on features (position, length, cue words) before decoding or assembling a summary. Encoder–decoder architectures were later employed in extractive-plus-brief-generation settings, but the defining trait remains a learning-based extraction step rather than text generation (Qi et al., 2022; , Koto et al., 2021).

ESG relevance and examples

In Indonesian ESG reporting, supervised extractive systems can learn to prioritize sentences that reliably cite governance structures, risk disclosures, and materiality statements, ensuring the selected content is directly linkable to source passages for audit trails. They can also be trained to respect pillar structure by incorporating features that indicate Environment, Social, or Governance content, improving content planning and traceability (Fei et al., 2022; , Maitama et al., 2020).

Strengths and caveats

Strengths: data-driven prioritization that can adapt to company-specific reporting styles; preserves exact figures and citations; relatively transparent decision criteria. Limits: requires substantial labeled ESG data for training, may overfit to training corpora and fail to generalize across sectors or languages, and still does not generate new prose or reframe evidence for readability without additional decoding steps (Qi et al., 2022; , Koto et al., 2021).
3.4 Neural Abstractive Summarization with Pretraining

Definition and architecture

Neural abstractive summarization uses encoder–decoder architectures to generate concise, fluent summaries that may rephrase or compress source content. Pretrained seq2seq models (e.g., BART, PEGASUS) adapted to document-level or domain-specific tasks can produce highly coherent summaries and user-friendly narratives; fine-tuning on ESG corpora enables domain adaptation. At a high level, these models learn to compress and rewrite content rather than copying verbatim passages (Qi et al., 2022; , Koto et al., 2021).
ESG relevance and examples

Abstractive methods are attractive for ESG reporting because they can synthesize Environment, Social, and Governance content into narrative statements, reduce redundancy, and present comparative insights across companies or years. However, integrity and traceability must be enforced, e.g., by grounding generation in explicit evidence passages and including citations to source documents to avoid fabrications about KPIs or governance claims (Fei et al., 2022; , Maitama et al., 2020), (Qi et al., 2022; .
Strengths and limitations

Strengths: fluent, cohesive, and domain-adaptable summaries; potential to produce pillar-structured narratives and cross-document syntheses; scalable to long ESG reports with appropriate training. Limits: risks of hallucination or misquoting figures; OCR-induced errors can propagate through generation; require robust grounding mechanisms and evaluation for factual consistency in ESG contexts (Fei et al., 2022; , Zhang et al., 2023; , Maitama et al., 2020), (Qi et al., 2022; .
3.5 Long-Document Summarization

Definition and challenges

Long-document summarization addresses documents with extensive length by employing hierarchical encoding (paragraph–section–document) or retrieval-augmented mechanisms to manage context. Techniques include hierarchical transformers, segment-wise processing with memory, and retrieval-augmented generation to fetch relevant passages during decoding. This is essential for ESG reports that span many pages, tables, and multi-year data Sari & Fitriani, 2024; , Goot et al., 2021).
ESG relevance and examples

Indonesian sustainability reports often exceed typical token limits and integrate tables of KPI data, governance disclosures, and stakeholder commentary. Long-document strategies enable pillar-aligned summaries by preserving context at multiple levels (section-level summaries feeding into a cross-sectional ESG brief) and by enabling retrieval of source passages to support each claim for auditability (Fei et al., 2022; , Maitama et al., 2020).
Strengths and limitations

Strengths: capacity to handle entire reports and cross-year documents; supports cross-document grounding and traceability; can incorporate retrieval to ensure evidence-backed claims. Limits: higher computational cost; complexity of maintaining coherent pillar structure across sections; OCR noise accumulates across long inputs if not properly preprocessed Maitama et al., 2020), Sari & Fitriani, 2024; .
3.6 Constrained / Controlled Summarization

Definition and objectives

Constrained or controlled summarization injects explicit constraints into generation or extraction, such as content plans, mandatory citations, or evidence validation steps. This includes planning content structure (e.g., a predefined Environment–Social–Governance outline), enforcing explicit source citations, or requiring validation against primary documents before final output. This approach aims to constrain outputs to be faithful to source evidence and conform to governance requirements (Qi et al., 2022; , Koto et al., 2021).
ESG relevance and examples

For Indonesian ESG disclosures, constrained summarization ensures that each claimed KPI or governance assertion is anchored to a source passage or data table, supports cross-document evidence trails, and adheres to regulatory expectations for auditable reporting. Content plans can enforce pillar-specific coverage and ensure that summaries do not omit critical governance disclosures or misrepresent KPI baselines. Validation steps can include automated cross-checks against source documents or regulatory language to reduce misstatement risk (Fei et al., 2022; , Maitama et al., 2020).
Strengths and implementation notes

Strengths: improves trustworthiness by design; facilitates auditability and regulatory alignment; supports governance-ready outputs with explicit citations. Limits: may reduce naturalness or conciseness; requires well-defined constraints and reliable grounding data; may demand more complex pipelines to enforce constraints across multi-document inputs (Fei et al., 2022; , Zhang et al., 2023; , Maitama et al., 2020).
Cross-cutting considerations for Indonesian ESG summarization

OCR resilience and multilingual handling: Across methods, robust preprocessing and grounding mechanisms are essential to prevent propagation of recognition errors into summaries, particularly for Indonesian reports that mix Indonesian and English passages and include tables and figures (Fei et al., 2022; , Degife & Lin, 2024; , Chebolu et al., 2023).
Evidence grounding and citation anchors: ESG contexts require explicit anchoring of claims to sources, including KPI tables, governance documents, and certifications, regardless of whether the summarization is extractive or abstractive (Fei et al., 2022; , Zhang et al., 2023; , Maitama et al., 2020).
Pillar-structured outputs: A recurring design goal is to align outputs with ESG pillars, enabling straightforward comparisons across companies and over time; this can be facilitated by content planning or structure-aware models at both extractive and abstractive stages (Fei et al., 2022; , Maitama et al., 2020).
Notes on potential combinations

Hybrid pipelines that combine extractive grounding with abstractive generation (e.g., extractive sentence selection followed by constrained abstractive rewriting with citation anchors) offer a practical path to both readability and fidelity in Indonesian ESG disclosures. Retrieval-augmented long-document architectures can further enhance coverage while preserving evidence trails (Qi et al., 2022; , Sari & Fitriani, 2024; , Goot et al., 2021).
