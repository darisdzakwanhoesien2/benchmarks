Section 6. Faithfulness: Sources of Errors and Mitigation Strategies

This section analyzes how faithfulness and factual accuracy can fail in Indonesian ESG summarization and delineates practical strategies to mitigate these errors. We categorize common error modes in abstractive summaries, then map concrete mitigations to the ESG domain. The focus is on evidence-first design, explicit content planning, citation anchoring, post-hoc validation, human audits, and OCR confidence filtering to preserve auditability and trust in summaries.

6.1 Common Error Modes in Abstractive Summaries

Entity-level misidentification

Definition: The generated summary substitutes, confuses, or misattributes ESG entities (e.g., confusing a company’s board with another firm, or misnaming a policy, standard, or KPI). In ESG contexts, such errors undermine governance traceability and can mislead stakeholders about who is responsible for a given commitment or outcome.
ESG example: A summary states that “Company X reduced GHG emissions by 20% in 2023,” while the source KPI table shows “Company Y reduced emissions by 20% in 2022.” Such an entity flip destroys evidentiary linkage and auditability.
Numeric derailment

Definition: Numerical values, units, baselines, dates, or targets are corrupted, rounded incorrectly, or mismatched to their corresponding statements.
ESG example: A narrative claims “emissions intensity improved by 15%” but the KPI table reports “EMISSION INTENSITY (tCO2e per unit of product) improved by 0.15 (not 15%),” or the year reference is inconsistent (2022 vs 2023). Numeric fidelity is critical in ESG due to materiality judgments and regulatory expectations.
Scope and boundary errors

Definition: The summary overextends or underfits the coverage of claims, either pulling in peripheral content or omitting material statements (e.g., excluding governance risks while including minor environmental anecdotes).
ESG example: A short summary mentions energy efficiency improvements but omits the related scope 1/2 emission disclosures and the regulatory context, thereby misrepresenting the operational boundary of the displayed KPI.
Temporal misalignment

Definition: The temporal framing of claims (year, period, cadence) mismatches source documents, leading to misleading conclusions about performance trajectories.
ESG example: A claim that “the company achieved targets in 2023” when the underlying target is set for 2024–2025 or the year in the source is 2022.
Causal overreach and causal misattribution

Definition: The summary infers causal links (e.g., “policy X caused KPI Y improvement”) without robust supporting evidence, or it attributes causality to mechanisms that are not explicitly evidenced in the primary disclosures.
ESG example: “Adoption of renewable energy policy caused a 10% reduction in emissions” when the document only reports correlation or lacks a causal analysis linking the policy to the observed outcome.
Cross-document inconsistency

Definition: When multiple source documents contain conflicting claims, the generated summary reconciles them incorrectly or fabricates a consensus that does not exist.
ESG example: Governance statements across years disagree on board independence metrics; the summary presents a harmonized view without acknowledging the discrepancy.
Hallucinations of regulatory or standards references

Definition: The summary cites standards, guidelines, or regulatory language that do not appear in the sources or that are misquoted.
ESG example: A summary cites “POJK 51/2020” as a mandatory disclosure obligation for a specific KPI, whereas the source documents reference a different or non-mandatory standard.
Linguistic and formatting artifacts

Definition: Generated text inherits OCR-induced artifacts that distort meaning or splits or merges data tables, making later extraction of evidence more difficult.
ESG example: A table caption or captioned KPI is garbled, leading to a narrative claim that the “energy intensity” is a unit that cannot be matched to the source, impairing traceability.
6.2 Practical Mitigations

Evidence-first design and content planning

Prioritize anchoring every claim to explicit evidence.
Implement a content-plan phase that specifies pillar coverage (Environment, Social, Governance), target KPIs, and the exact evidence types to use (tables, policy clauses, minutes, certifications) before generation or extraction begins.
Use a structured template that maps each claim to a source fragment, enabling downstream verification and auditability.
Content planning and citation anchors

Enforce pre-declared citation anchors for all claims, including precise location tags (e.g., “KPI table, row 4, column C” or “Policy clause POJK-XYZ, Section 2.3”).
Maintain a citation ledger that records source passages, their token positions, and their confidence scores to support traceability through the entire pipeline.
Post-hoc validation and factual checks

Integrate post-hoc verification stages where generated outputs are checked against primary sources using:
NLI-based entailment checks against the source passages.
QA-based verification by answering targeted questions derived from the summary and verifying congruence with the corresponding source data.
Domain-specific classifiers trained to detect typical ESG misstatements (e.g., mismatched KPI units, misattributed governance statements).
Implement a rejection or abstention mechanism when verification fails, signaling the need for human review rather than presenting a partially grounded result.
OCR confidence filtering and grounding

Use OCR confidence scores to modulate reliance on particular passages. For passages with low confidence, privilege cross-checking with other corroborating sources (e.g., multiple tables or governance documents) or automatic extraction from higher-quality PDFs when available.
Implement layout-aware extraction pipelines to preserve anchor points (tables, headers, section titles) that are crucial for evidence grounding.
Human audits and sampling strategies

Regularly conduct targeted human audits on a stratified sample of summaries, focusing on high-risk content (KPI values, regulatory references, and governance claims) to assess fidelity and grounding.
Use audit results to refine both the content-planning templates and the grounding rules, feeding back into model fine-tuning and evaluation criteria.
Evidence-grounding and dashboards

Build an evidence-grounding ledger that links each claim to its source fragment with a reliability score and a quick provenance trace (document name, year, page/section, and data table).
Provide dashboards for auditors and investors that display:
Pillar-wise coverage and grounding density.
Percentage of claims with explicit citations.
OCR-noise levels and their impact on grounding stability.
Discrepancies or conflicts across documents and years, with traceable explanations.
OCR-aware evaluation and data cleaning

Before evaluation, perform OCR-aware cleaning to correct common misreads, especially numerical values and table structures, to restore unit consistency and baselines.
Include an explicit step in data preprocessing to align KPI units and baselines across years, reducing downstream mismatches during evaluation.
Operational considerations and governance

Maintain separation between the generation model and the grounding module to minimize propagation of hallucinations. The generator can propose candidate text, but all factual assertions require a grounding pass.
Document decisions and constraints (e.g., allowed paraphrasing limits, mandatory citations, and acceptable levels of deviation in KPI reporting) to support regulator-facing transparency.
Synthesis and guidance for researchers

Adopt a multi-layered faithfulness strategy combining evidence-first planning, procedural grounding, post-hoc validation, and human oversight.
Emphasize explicit citations and source-proximate generation (i.e., generation that mirrors exact source phrases when feasible, followed by constrained paraphrasing that preserves the evidentiary link).
Treat OCR confidence as a first-class signal in both data preparation and evaluation, recognizing its pivotal role in driving evidence-grounded outputs.
