Section 5. Evaluation of Summarization

This section presents a multi-dimensional evaluation framework for Indonesian ESG disclosures. It argues that ROUGE alone is insufficient for ESG needs and outlines a protocol that combines overlap-based, semantic, faithfulness, human, and benchmark-driven assessments to yield trustworthy, evidence-grounded summaries.

5.1 Overlap-Based Metrics (ROUGE)

Rationale for use

Overlap-based metrics, notably ROUGE, have long been the default for quick, reproducible benchmarking of salience and n-gram recovery against expert summaries. They provide a familiar, automated signal for early-stage method comparison and enable rapid iteration in ESG-focused pipelines where timing is critical for investor updates and regulatory readiness (Qi et al., 2022; , Koto et al., 2021; , Rafea et al., 2021).
Limitations for ESG

In ESG disclosures, high ROUGE scores do not guarantee factual fidelity or evidentiary grounding. ESG content frequently contains precise KPI values, citations, and regulatory language where small deviations or paraphrasing can misrepresent a claim even if ROUGE remains high. Moreover, boilerplate governance statements and cross-year data points can inflate overlap without reflecting materiality or source-level correctness. Consequently, ROUGE can be misaligned with auditability needs and cross-source consistency in ESG contexts (Fei et al., 2022; , Zhang et al., 2023; , Maitama et al., 2020), (Qi et al., 2022; .
Practical stance

Use ROUGE as a baseline diagnostic and for comparability with prior Indonesian summarization work, but interpret ROUGE with caution in ESG tasks. Report complementary metrics and provide error analyses that reveal grounding and factual fidelity issues rather than relying on ROUGE as a sole success criterion (Fei et al., 2022; , Maitama et al., 2020), Goot et al., 2021).
5.2 Semantic Similarity Metrics

What they add

Semantic similarity measures (e.g., BERTScore, SAF, or embedding-based cosine similarities) capture contextual similarity beyond exact n-gram overlap. They can better reflect content coverage when paraphrasing is used, which is common in abstractive ESG summaries that still need to preserve meaning and evidentiary intent (Qi et al., 2022; , [57], [58].
What they miss

Semantic similarity alone does not guarantee alignment to source evidence. A summary may semantically match the target content while still misrepresenting the specific KPI figures, citation anchors, or regulatory references. Without grounding supervision, semantic metrics may overlook factual misstatements or misattributions that are critical for ESG audits (Fei et al., 2022; , Maitama et al., 2020).
Practical stance

Employ semantic similarity alongside ROUGE to assess paraphrase quality and coverage, but couple with evidence-grounding checks (see 5.3) to ensure claims map to actual sources. Report both overlap- and semantic-based scores to illustrate complementary strengths and weaknesses (Fei et al., 2022; , Maitama et al., 2020).
5.3 Faithfulness and Factual Consistency Evaluation

Rationale

Faithfulness (factual accuracy) is central to ESG summaries because stakeholders require that outputs reflect verifiable evidence, not merely plausible text. In ESG contexts, hallucinations or misquotations can undermine investor trust and regulatory compliance. Partial or misaligned grounding undermines the utility of summaries for due diligence and audit trails, even if stylistic quality is high (Fei et al., 2022; , Zhang et al., 2023; , Maitama et al., 2020).
Methods

Natural Language Inference (NLI): Treat summaries as hypotheses about source evidence and evaluate whether each claim is entailed, contradicted, or neutral with respect to the primary disclosures.
Question-Answering (QA) grounded checks: Generate targeted questions (e.g., “What is the reported GHG emission intensity for year X?”) and verify that the summary provides correct answers supported by source passages.
Classifier-based factual checks: Train domain-specific classifiers to detect incorrect KPI reporting, misattribution of governance claims, or misalignment with cited standards (GRI, POJK, etc.).
Evidence-grounding evaluation: Assess whether each claim in the summary cites a source passage, table, or external document and whether the cited evidence is sufficiently proximate to support the claim.
ESG considerations

For Indonesian ESG disclosures, design evaluation prompts around pillar-specific evidence ( Environment KPI, Social programs, Governance structures) and require citation anchors in the output. Where possible, test cross-document consistency (e.g., a KPI stated in a KPI table should be traceable to that table) and verify unit correctness and baselines. Collect human judgments on whether the grounded evidence accurately supports each claim (Fei et al., 2022; , Zhang et al., 2023; , Maitama et al., 2020).
5.4 Human Evaluation

Axes and protocol

Faithfulness: Do the summaries accurately reflect the content and evidentiary basis? Are numerical values and regulatory phrases exact or correctly paraphrased without altering meaning?
Coverage: Are all ESG pillars adequately represented? Are material claims, governance references, and material risks included?
Usefulness: Is the summary actionable for investors, auditors, or regulators? Does it enable efficient retrieval of source passages?
Auditability: Are there explicit citations or anchors to primary documents to permit traceability?
Procedures

Recruit domain-expert raters (e.g., ESG analysts, auditors, or corporate governance researchers) to assess a sample of summaries on the above axes using Likert scales and structured checklists.
Include a grounded evaluation component where raters verify a subset of claims against source passages and indicate any discrepancies.
Report inter-annotator agreement (e.g., Cohen’s kappa) to reflect consistency in domain judgments, and provide qualitative error analyses highlighting recurrent grounding pitfalls or misstatements.
ESG-specific considerations

Given Indonesian disclosures’ mix of Indonesian and English, ensure raters are proficient in both languages or provide dual-language evaluation intervals. Emphasize the auditability and evidence-grounding aspects to align with regulatory expectations (Fei et al., 2022; , Maitama et al., 2020).
5.5 Evaluation Under a Benchmark Mindset

Reproducibility and transparency

Publish standardized evaluation configurations, data splits (train/validation/test by year and instrument type), and preprocessing steps (OCR post-processing, table extraction, and pillar tagging rules). Provide code, seeds, and exact model versions to enable replication.
Define a canonical evaluation protocol that includes:
A baseline (heuristic extractive) and a strong abstractive model.
A multi-metric reporting package (ROUGE-1/2/L, BERTScore, grounding-based metrics, and a faithfulness score from NLI/QA checks).
A grounding audit metric (percentage of claims with explicit source anchors).
Dashboards and reporting

Develop dashboards that summarize performance across pillars, documents, and years, with drill-down capabilities to verify source passages. Include visuals for fidelity vs. scarcity of OCR noise, evidence grounding coverage, and cross-document consistency trends.
Include an evidence-grounding ledger showing, for each claim, the claimed evidence passage, its location, and its reliability score, to support investor and regulator reviews.
Practical guidelines

Do not rely on a single metric; adopt a multi-dimensional protocol that reflects ESG needs for accuracy, accountability, and traceability. When reporting results, clearly state limitations of each metric, especially ROUGE in ESG contexts, and provide diagnostic analyses that explain why certain summaries succeed or fail in terms of grounding and auditability (Fei et al., 2022; , Maitama et al., 2020).
Synthesis and recommended protocol

A recommended ESG-oriented evaluation protocol combines:
Overlap metrics (ROUGE) for baseline comparability, with explicit caveats.
Semantic similarity to capture paraphrase quality.
Faithfulness checks (NLI/QA/classifiers) to assess factual consistency.
Human evaluation focusing on faithfulness, coverage, usefulness, and auditability.
Benchmark-minded reproducibility artifacts (config files, data splits, dashboards, and grounding traces).
Avoid asserting metric correlations unless empirically justified in the targeted ESG corpus; instead, report each metric independently and provide cross-metric analyses as exploratory insights.
