Section 2. Background: Summarization Task Definitions

This section defines core summarization tasks and clarifies how they map to Indonesian ESG disclosures, with a focus on pillar-structured summaries and evidence traceability. Each subsection ties the general definitions to ESG reporting needs such as preserving KPI fidelity, governance references, and auditable sources.

2.1 Extractive vs. Abstractive Summarization

Definition and core distinction

Extractive summarization selects and concatenates sentences or passages directly from the input document to form a shorter version, without generating new text. Abstractive summarization, in contrast, generates novel sentences that paraphrase and compress the content, potentially reformulating claims and reorganizing information. This dichotomy is foundational for ESG contexts where accuracy and traceability of evidence are paramount Qi et al., 2022), (Sari & Fitriani, 2024; , Goot et al., 2021).

Practical ESG considerations

Given OCR noise and multilingual Indonesian disclosures, extractive methods offer higher immediate factual fidelity since they reuse exact language, which aids traceability to the source. However, abstractive methods are needed for user-friendly, decision-focused dashboards that highlight material ESG claims and their evidentiary basis; thus, a hybrid or guided abstractive approach is often desirable, provided factual grounding is enforced through evidence grounding and citation anchors (Fei et al., 2022; , Zhang et al., 2023; , Maitama et al., 2020; .
2.2 Single-Document vs. Multi-Document Summarization

Definition and scope

Single-document summarization creates a concise representation of one ESG disclosure document (e.g., one annual sustainability report). Multi-document summarization aggregates content from a set of related ESG documents (e.g., annual reports, sustainability reports, governance policies, certification letters) to produce a unified summary that reflects cross-document coherence and coverage. The distinction is central for ESG due diligence and investor briefings where materiality emerges across multiple sources Qi et al., 2022), (Sari & Fitriani, 2024; , Koto et al., 2021; .
ESG implications and examples

A single Indonesian sustainability report may describe governance structures and KPI outcomes; a multi-document summary would synthesize governance disclosures across multiple years or cross-reference with certifications (e.g., ISO, environmental permits) and stakeholder engagement records to present a traceable risk and opportunity profile. This is important for evidence-grounded summaries that must align statements with source documents across documents, enhancing reliability for audits and investor messaging (Fei et al., 2022; , Maitama et al., 2020; , Alshaikh et al., 2024; , Yao et al., 2020).
Evidence traceability in ESG

Multi-document summarization in ESG contexts should preserve linkages to each source passage or table (e.g., “see KPI table on page 42, row 7”), enabling auditors to verify the claims. This motivates the integration of citation anchors and cross-document grounding in the summarization process to support accountability and regulator scrutiny (Fei et al., 2022; , Zhang et al., 2023; , Maitama et al., 2020; .
2.3 Query-Focused and Structured Summarization

Definition and purpose

Query-focused summarization generates summaries tailored to a specific information need or stakeholder query (e.g., “What are the major environmental risks and mitigation measures?”). Structured summarization organizes content into predefined ESG pillars (Environment, Social, Governance) and possibly subtopics (e.g., energy intensity, supply chain labor practices, board independence), producing pillar-structured outputs that align with ESG reporting frameworks and rating criteria.
ESG implications and examples

For Indonesian ESG disclosures, a query such as “Environmental performance and evidence of reduction targets” would guide the summarizer to extract and condense relevant KPI results, policy commitments, and cited evidence. Structured summaries facilitate quick comparisons across companies and time periods, supporting materiality assessments and regulator reviews. They also enable traceability by directly anchoring each claim to the associated evidence (data tables, policy clauses, certification statements) within the output (Fei et al., 2022; , Lv et al., 2023; , Maitama et al., 2020; .
Implications for trust and governance

Query-focused and structured outputs support decision-makers by delivering evidence-grounded narratives that map to ESG materiality categories and corresponding indicators, reducing interpretation ambiguity and enabling straightforward audit trails (Fei et al., 2022; , Zhang et al., 2023; , Maitama et al., 2020; .
2.4 Query-Focused Multi-Document Summarization

Definition and rationale

This task extends query-focused summarization to a corpus of related ESG documents, producing a concise, evidence-grounded synthesis tuned to a stakeholder query. The challenge lies in aligning salient points across documents while avoiding contradictions and preserving cross-document evidence trails. In ESG contexts, this is essential for regulatory inquiries, investor due diligence, and cross-year trend analysis, where stakeholders expect coherent narratives backed by explicit sources (Sari & Fitriani, 2024; , Koto et al., 2021; , Goot et al., 2021).
ESG implications and examples

Consider an Indonesian company with multiple years of sustainability reports, governance memos, and third-party assurance letters. A query like “What governance improvements have been implemented and what is the evidentiary basis (policies, board minutes, certifications)?” would require the system to pull statements from multiple sources, reconcile any inconsistencies, and present a unified, source-backed summary. Such a task supports auditability, materiality tracing, and transparent stakeholder communication. The outputs should include explicit citations to sources and, where feasible, passages or data tables that substantiate each claim (Fei et al., 2022; , Zhang et al., 2023; , Maitama et al., 2020; , Qi et al., 2022).
Evidence-grounded design considerations

OCR resilience, multilingual text handling, and structured data in ESG reports demand approaches that (a) retain citation anchors to primary documents, (b) preserve quantitative figures with their units and baselines, and (c) support cross-document inference without fabricating evidence. Approaches that couple abstractive generation with explicit grounding (e.g., extractive sentence selections augmented with generation, or generation conditioned on source passages) are aligned with the need for trustworthiness in ESG contexts (Fei et al., 2022; , Zhang et al., 2023; , Maitama et al., 2020; , Qi et al., 2022).
Cross-cutting notes on alignment with standards

Across all four definitions, the common thread is evidence traceability: every claim in a summary should be anchored to a source passage, KPI table, governance document, or certification. This ties directly to ESG reporting needs for governance, risk management, and investor trust. OCR noise and multilingual content in Indonesian disclosures intensify the necessity for robust grounding, document-layout-aware processing, and cross-document citation mechanisms to maintain fidelity and auditability (Fei et al., 2022; , Degife & Lin, 2024; , Chebolu et al., 2023; , Maitama et al., 2020; .
Synthesis and implications for the review

The taxonomy above provides a framework for evaluating summarization approaches in Indonesian ESG disclosures, guiding methodological choices toward extractive, abstractive, or hybrid methods with explicit grounding. It also informs the evaluation design by highlighting the need for pillar-structured outputs, source citation, and cross-document consistency to meet trust and regulatory requirements in ESG communication (Fei et al., 2022; , Zhang et al., 2023; , Maitama et al., 2020; , Qi et al., 2022).
