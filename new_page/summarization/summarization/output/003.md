Section 4. Datasets and Domain Considerations

This section outlines canonical and domain-specific data considerations for Indonesian ESG summarization, with attention to multilingual and low-resource realities, OCR-induced noise, and practical implications for experimental design. We discuss how ESG disclosures differ from news, including structure, boilerplate, numeric density, and auditability, and address Indonesian-language challenges, code-switching, and domain terminology. We also describe OCR error types and their propagation into summaries, and conclude with sampling, cleaning, and evidence-selection practices that support robust evaluation and reproducibility.

4.1 Canonical Summarization Datasets (and Why ESG Differs)

Overview of canonical datasets and relevance

Canonical summarization datasets (e.g., IndoSum, Liputan6-based Indonesian news corpora, and XL-SUM for Indonesian) have historically driven Indonesian single-document extractive and abstractive summarization research. These corpora typically consist of news articles with human-authored abstractive summaries or extractive references and have established baselines for ROUGE and related metrics (Rafea et al., 2021; , Yoko et al., 2018; , Mandar & Gunawan, 2017), Heever et al., 2024; , Cui et al., 2024; , Prabhune et al., 2024; , (Feitosa, n.d.; . However, ESG disclosures diverge from these datasets in several fundamental ways, impacting task formulation and evaluation.
How ESG differs from news and why this matters

Structure and modality: ESG disclosures are pillar-structured narratives that interleave narrative discussion with numerical KPIs, tables, certifications, and regulatory language. News datasets are predominantly continuous prose with less enforced structural segmentation, making direct transfer of news-focused baselines suboptimal for ESG tasks (Mishra et al., 2026; , Sheth, 2025; , Uchendu et al., 2021).
Repetitiveness and boilerplate: ESG reports frequently contain boilerplate language (e.g., governance statements, risk disclosures, policy boilerplate) repeated across years and companies, which can skew extractive baselines toward boilerplate passages and obscure material variability across entities (Vu et al., 2022; , Lee et al., 2021).
Numeric density and fidelity: ESG documents embed dense KPI data, baselines, targets, and units that must be preserved with exactitude. Summarization models must maintain numerical fidelity and unit consistency, which is less critical in typical news summaries (Fei et al., 2022; , Maitama et al., 2020; , Mandar & Gunawan, 2017).
Auditability and evidence grounding: ESG outputs require traceability to source passages, data tables, and regulatory phrases to support audits and regulator scrutiny; canonical news datasets lack built-in mechanisms for source anchoring and cross-document evidence trails that are essential in ESG contexts (Fei et al., 2022; , Zhang et al., 2023; , Maitama et al., 2020; .
Implications: ESG-focused evaluation should extend beyond conventional ROUGE-like metrics to incorporate factual fidelity, numerical accuracy, and source-citation groundedness. It also motivates domain-specific fine-tuning on Indonesian ESG corpora and the integration of evidence-grounding supervision during training (Fei et al., 2022; , Maitama et al., 2020; , Qi et al., 2022).
Indonesian-specific dataset landscape and gaps

Indonesian single-document summarization has historically leaned toward extractive methods due to data scarcity and the need for interpretability in governance contexts. Large-scale Indonesian ESG-relevant corpora are comparatively limited, with few public benchmarks that capture cross-year sustainability disclosures, governance memos, and certifications in a unified format (Mishra et al., 2026; , [53], (Feitosa, n.d.; .
Multi-document ESG summarization remains underexplored in Indonesian, where cross-document synthesis must reconcile KPI tables, governance directives, and stakeholder engagement evidence across documents and years, creating a need for retrieval-augmented, citation-grounded approaches (Sari & Fitriani, 2024; , Goot et al., 2021).
Practical implications for dataset construction

When building ESG-focused benchmarks, include: (a) pillar-aligned excerpts (Environment, Social, Governance), (b) explicit KPI tables with units and baselines, (c) governance passages with references to board structures or policies, and (d) evidence passages with citation anchors linking to source documents.
Include cross-year and cross-document variants to facilitate longitudinal analysis and materiality tracing, enabling evaluation of cross-document consistency and evidence grounding.
Encourage multi-source assembly that mimics investor-facing ESG briefs, requiring summaries to integrate content from annual reports, sustainability reports, governance memos, assurance letters, and certification documents.
4.2 Multilingual and Low-Resource Constraints (Indonesian)

Language and data-resource context

Indonesian is underrepresented in NLP benchmarks relative to its vast speaker base, despite being a major global language. Foundational resources include IndoBERT, IndoBERTweet, IndoBART, and related Indonesian benchmarks (e.g., INDOLEM, INDObERTWEET) that enable domain adaptation but still face domain gaps when shifting to ESG disclosures that mix Indonesian with English and domain-specific terms (Mishra et al., 2026; , [54], (Feitosa, n.d.; , [55].
Code-switching and domain terms

Indonesian ESG disclosures often exhibit code-switching to English for technical terms, international standards (e.g., GRI, ISO), and regulatory phrases; domain terminology includes acronyms such as SPOTT, POJK, IDX, GHG, carbon intensity, and governance constructs. This multilingual, code-switched milieu complicates tokenization, named entity recognition, and KB grounding, and amplifies the need for tokenization schemes and lexica that cover both Indonesian and English ESG vocabulary (Mishra et al., 2026; , [56], (Feitosa, n.d.; .
Low-resource signaling and annotation challenges

Few publicly available large-scale ESG-specific Indonesian annotation sets exist, complicating supervised training for argument extraction, evidence grounding, and cross-document summarization tasks. This scarcity motivates data-efficient strategies, such as domain-adaptive pretraining on ESG corpora, utilization of retrieval-augmented generation, and semi-supervised or weakly supervised evidence grounding signals (Feitosa, n.d.; , [55].
Practical implications for experimental design

Data collection: assemble ESG disclosures from IDX-listed Indonesian firms, including annual reports, sustainability reports, governance statements, and assurance letters. Where possible, align data collection with regulatory timeframes (annual reporting cycles) to support longitudinal analysis.
Preprocessing: deploy multilingual tokenizers and domain-specific lexicons to handle Indonesian-English code-switching, abbreviations, and domain acronyms; implement layout-aware parsing to preserve table content and section headers critical for pillar alignment.
Annotation and weak supervision: leverage weakly supervised labeling for pillar tagging, KPI extraction, and evidence-citing phrases, supplemented by a limited set of human-annotated ESG samples to bootstrap domain-adaptive models.
Model adaptation: fine-tune Indonesian pre-trained models (e.g., IndoBERT, IndoBART) on ESG-specific corpora, and consider retrieval-augmented generation to improve evidence grounding when training data is limited.
4.3 OCR and Table-to-Text Noise

OCR error typology and propagation

Indonesian ESG disclosures are frequently distributed across scanned PDFs, image-based slides, or multi-column layouts, leading to OCR-induced errors such as character misrecognition, line segmentation errors, table structure misinterpretation, and misalignment of headers with data cells. Common error types include: misrecognized numerals (e.g., 1 vs l, 0 vs O), broken hyphenation, dropped punctuation, missing diacritics, and mis-OCR of table borders or shading. When such errors enter the text stream, they propagate into summarization as corrupted tokens, misread KPI values, and garbled references, undermining both grammatical quality and factual fidelity (Fei et al., 2022; , Degife & Lin, 2024; , Chebolu et al., 2023).
Impact on downstream summarization

OCR noise destabilizes entity recognition, table extraction, and cross-reference linking, impeding evidence grounding. It can distort numerical values (e.g., emissions figures and baselines), mislabel sections, and obscure citation anchors, which in turn degrades both extractive selectivity and abstractive generation quality.
Table-to-text conversion is particularly prone to misalignment: when a KPI table row is misread, the corresponding narrative claim may no longer align with the correct unit, period, or baseline, creating audit-risk in ESG summaries.
Practical implications for experimental design

Preprocessing and cleaning: include robust layout-aware OCR post-processing, table-structure recovery, and error correction pipelines. Techniques may combine OCR confidence scores, heuristic table reconstruction, and domain-specific post-editing rules to restore data fidelity before summarization.
Evidence selection and grounding: design grounding strategies that tolerate occasional OCR errors by prioritizing high-confidence evidence anchors, and by including multiple corroborating sources (e.g., cross-check KPI data with governance statements). Implement fallback mechanisms to query source passages when confidence is low.
Sampling strategies: implement stratified sampling across documents with varying OCR quality (e.g., scanned older reports vs. modern digitally generated PDFs) to understand robustness, and ensure that evaluation sets cover a spectrum of OCR noise levels.
Noise-aware evaluation: augment automatic metrics with human verification on critical KPI values and citations to ensure factual fidelity, and incorporate metrics that measure evidence-grounding quality under OCR noise (e.g., citation recall, grounding accuracy).
Reproducibility: document OCR pipelines, text-cleaning steps, and evidence-grounding rules to enable reproducible experiments and transparent comparisons across ESG summarization methods.
Cross-cutting considerations

Domain-specific evaluation framework: combine pillar-aligned summaries with strict evidence anchors, enabling auditors and investors to trace claims back to their sources even when OCR noise is present.
Memory and efficiency: long ESG reports with tables require chunking and hierarchical processing; OCR noise can complicate segmentation, reinforcing the need for robust preprocessing to maintain semantic coherence across segments.
