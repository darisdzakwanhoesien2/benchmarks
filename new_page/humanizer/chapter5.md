This thesis's main point is that analyzing ESG disclosures in Indonesian sustainability reports becomes much more useful when we don't just treat it as a way to find topics. Instead, it's better to see it as a process of extracting specific evidence, paying attention to the tone, keeping track of its origin, and aligning it with an ontology. Chapter 4's findings support this argument. The workflow we used successfully turned 23 sustainability report PDFs into a searchable collection of about 5,512 pages, processed by OCR. From this, we created 332 ESG records that showed a specific tone and 2,074 T2 rows. We also matched 52 different aspects to their corresponding ontology paths, achieving an 83.7% agreement with ClimateBERT-style commitment labels, with a Cohen’s kappa of 0.645.

The key empirical finding is that the tone of a disclosure can be measured and is meaningful. Most of the evidence we pulled out showed a 'commitment' tone – 115 out of 332 records. Environmental disclosure also appeared most often, with 179 records. This tells us that the corporate disclosures we looked at aren't mainly focused on actual, proven results. Instead, they focus on future promises and how companies want to position themselves. This pattern is crucial because it directly supports why we started this thesis: just looking at positive or negative sentiment wouldn't tell us the difference between something good that was achieved and a good promise for the future.

Among the options we tested for our thesis, the best setup involved using prompts that understood tone, combined with models that consistently followed a set structure. Specifically, arcee-ai/trinity-large-preview:free, when used with prompts designed for specific tones, worked best. This setup didn't just generate more text; it created records that were actually usable, with very few instances of missing tone or structural inconsistencies compared to other options. On the other hand, openai/gpt-oss-120b:free parsed everything perfectly in a smaller sample, but it completely missed the tone in all cases and showed a 42.5% rate of structural drift in the larger group. This result clearly shows that for this kind of task, following the defined structure and capturing the tone are more important than how big or generally fluent a model is.

So, the most important technical takeaway from Chapter 4 isn't that one specific model was superior. Instead, it's that how you design the prompts and how the model behaves together is the key. Our thesis initially assumed that how sensitive the prompts were and how easily structured outputs could break would largely determine quality. Chapter 4's results confirmed this. They also revealed that covering all the terms in our ontology was fairly stable, but assigning tone was the system's weakest point. This both confirms and adds more detail to what we discussed in Chapter 3. Chapter 3 expected different parts of the system to have weaknesses; Chapter 4 shows that the biggest problem is with keeping tone assignments consistent, not just with OCR or mapping to the ontology.

### 5.1.2 Deeper Patterns and Research Question Mapping

Looking deeper, we see that different parts of the system need different types of information to work. The OCR part relies on having complete documents and being able to trace information back to specific pages. The extraction part depends on how we phrase the prompts, how well the model follows instructions, and if the output structure stays consistent. The comparison part needs to see how much the tone signals overlap with climate-topic signals. Finally, the ontology part requires good coverage of ESG terms. Each of these components can fail for different reasons, which highlights why a modular design is important.

The most important part of our entire process is how we designed the prompts to be aware of tone. You can see this clearly in the prompt stability table: a basic data.md prompt could be parsed, but it was bad at capturing tone. In contrast, 'chain-of-thought' tone prompts produced many more records and had fewer omissions. This tells us that the best results from our pipeline don't come from a model's general ability to extract information, but from giving it clear instructions that make it distinguish between commitments, actions, and outcomes.

Different methods also depend on different kinds of information. For example, the rule-based model looks for specific words like 'berkomitmen' (committed), 'will', 'target', 'achieved', and 'successfully'. The traditional TF-IDF model uses basic statistics about words and characters. The hybrid model is built to use information from the surrounding text and the ontology. But the LLM extraction part, on the other hand, relies on understanding the broader context and following instructions. This is why texts focused on governance or signing agreements can be tricky: they often contain both signs of action and commitment language, so simple word clues and general interpretations might not agree.

### 5.2 Research Question Resolution

### 5.2.1 Resolution of RQ1 and RQ2

Research Question 1 asked if we could turn sustainability report PDFs into organized ESG evidence using our system. The answer is yes. Our OCR and preprocessing steps handled 23 documents, totaling about 5,512 pages. Afterward, the extraction part of the pipeline created a consistent store of evidence, with 332 records showing tone and 2,074 T2 outputs. What this means in practice is that the thesis has shown it's possible to convert raw reports into machine-readable ESG evidence, linked to specific pages, even with a large number of documents.

Research Question 2 asked if our system could describe ESG disclosures in more detail than just finding general topics, specifically by separating pillars, aspects, sentiment, and disclosure tone. The answer is also yes, but with an important note. Our results show meaningful patterns in both tone and ESG pillar, with 'commitment' being the most common tone and environmental disclosure appearing most often. This confirms that our framework can capture several different aspects of how companies disclose information. However, our initial human annotations and cases where the tone was missed indicate that this representation isn't equally dependable for all types of text. So, RQ2's contribution isn't just that such a framework exists, but that this thesis shows where it works well and where it needs more careful human input.

### 5.2.2 Resolution of RQ3 and RQ4

Research Question 3 asked how our tone-aware system compares to ClimateBERT-style climate classification. The answer is that they are closely related but not the same. Our agreement table shows an 83.7% match and a Cohen’s kappa of 0.645 between our 'commitment' tone labels and ClimateBERT's climate-commitment labels for 332 records. This is a strong finding because it confirms our tone classification system is relevant while also showing it's distinct. Practically speaking, ClimateBERT-style labels can tell us if a disclosure is about climate, but our tone labels can also tell us if it's a promise, an action, or an outcome.

Figure 5.2 helps visualize the overlap between these concepts more directly than just explaining it in text.

Research Question 4 asked what problems still exist in our current evaluation and extraction approach. The main issues are that tone is sometimes missed, the structure of the output can change unexpectedly, and the system struggles with vague or unclear text. The strongest proof for this includes 61 cases where tone was omitted, 19 cases of schema drift in predictions that were missed, failures with vague or uncertain language, and how much the results change depending on the prompt or model chosen. Our evaluation strategy is good enough to uncover these weaknesses because it combines OCR completion, parsing success, summaries of stability, disagreement tables, ontology coverage, and pilot review files. However, it's not yet enough to claim our system is definitively superior to benchmarks because we still have limited perfect, 'gold-standard' annotations. Overall, these four research questions demonstrate that we have achieved the thesis goal of creating an ESG disclosure analysis framework that works, can be understood, and offers rich diagnostic insights.

### 5.3 Limitations and Threats to Validity

The first big limitation is with our reference data. This thesis doesn't yet use a complete, expert-labeled dataset for comparison. Instead, we use a layered system based on extracted records, ClimateBERT-style labels for comparison, suggested rules, and initial human annotations. While this is good enough for exploring and understanding disagreements, it limits how strongly we can claim our model performs perfectly.

The second limitation is how focused our study is on a specific area and sample. Our findings come from Indonesian sustainability and annual reports, which often contain both Indonesian and English or mixed languages. This focus helps us be specific to that domain, but it means we can't immediately apply these results to other regulatory environments, different types of documents, or texts that are only in one language. Also, the 'social' aspect of ESG is not very common in the reports we looked at for this thesis, so our findings about tone and aspects are more influenced by environmental and governance disclosures.

The third limitation is how sensitive the system is to the models and prompts we use. The prompt stability table shows that even prompts that are technically correct can still result in unusable tone outputs. The model stability table shows that some models might parse everything perfectly but still fail to capture tone or deviate from the expected structure. This means our system isn't robust enough to just swap out different underlying components. Its performance relies on carefully matching the prompt design with the model's behavior.

The fourth limitation involves specific technical failures that impact how we interpret the results. The failure-mode table lists 61 cases where tone was missing, 19 cases where the structure drifted, failures with vague language, problems with table layouts, and issues with specific Indonesian regulatory terms. These failures don't completely invalidate our process, but they do mean that the findings from Chapter 4 should be understood as coming from a research system that is strong but still has imperfections.

The fifth limitation is that our greenwashing index is still based on simple rules. Ratios at the company level, like 73.0 for BEST-SR_2024 or 4.778 for vktr_ar_sr_2024, are interesting to analyze, but they haven't been checked against outside ESG ratings, actual enforcement actions, or human expert opinions. Because of this, the index should currently be seen as a tool for initial screening or discussion, not for making final judgments.

### 5.4 Future Work

### 5.4.1 Immediate Technical Extensions

The most immediate step is to grow and formalize our human annotation process. Our current initial annotation files should be expanded into a structured, expert-reviewed benchmark, including checks for agreement between different annotators. This is especially important for distinguishing between commitment and action, for disclosures heavy on governance, and for records currently flagged as 'needs review.' This directly addresses the weakest part of our evaluation.

The second immediate improvement is to make our tone-specific prompt engineering and structure enforcement stronger. Our results already indicate that chain-of-thought prompts designed for tone work better than general extraction prompts. The next step is to turn this finding into a more systematic way of creating prompts, including stricter checks for output validity, confidence scores for individual fields, and specific logic to re-run processes for outputs where tone is missing.

The third immediate improvement is to evaluate OCR quality. Our current system shows that OCR completion works, but it doesn't yet provide data on character error rate, how accurately it extracts tables, or baseline semantic quality at the page level. Adding these checks would help us tell the difference between noise from the initial OCR process and actual difficulty in classifying tone later on.

The fourth immediate improvement is to benchmark our system directly against ClimateBERT using the full set of extracted records. Our repository already allows for generating record batches for this purpose. Finishing this part of the pipeline would turn our current indirect comparison into a more robust external reference.

The fifth immediate improvement is to filter out non-ESG and accounting-policy text more effectively. Our initial annotations show cases where financial or accounting statements are correctly identified as 'none', but clearer filtering at the beginning of the process could save extraction resources and improve the quality of later steps.

### 5.4.2 Long-Term Research Roadmap

Long-term, this research could expand into a larger program on using computers to analyze sustainability disclosures, especially in settings with limited resources or multiple languages. One path is to train models: we could improve our hybrid contextual architecture by fine-tuning it with a larger corpus of Indonesian ESG texts labeled for tone. This would help the system learn tone differences more directly, instead of relying so much on prompts for extraction.

Another direction is to make the system more robust and able to work in different areas. We should test the framework on longer, less clean, and more varied reports across different sectors. Then, we could extend it to other regulatory environments beyond Indonesia. This would show if our tone classification and ontology structure are still useful in different reporting cultures.

A third direction involves research focused on deployment. Since our repository already keeps track of where information came from at the page level and stores useful artifacts, it could be turned into a review system for analysts, such as for regulators, ESG research teams, investors, or auditors. In this scenario, the main research question would change from 'Can the model classify?' to 'How should humans and models work together when the quality of evidence isn't certain?'

A fourth direction is research on graphs and combining knowledge. Our ontology and semantic export parts already suggest using graph-based queries and methods similar to GraphRAG. Future work could explore how organized ESG evidence, ontology paths, and information about the source page can help in tracing back retrieved information, rather than just providing static reports.

Together, these future directions create a clear plan: first, make annotation and benchmarking more stable; then, improve how robust the models are and the quality of OCR; next, expand across different domains and deployment situations; and finally, integrate the entire process into a wider research system focused on evidence.

### 5.5 Broader Impact

### 5.5.1 Practical and Societal Benefits

This research will most directly help ESG analysts, people who write sustainability reports, regulators, investors, and research teams focused on corporate accountability. For ESG analysts, our system makes it cheaper to go from hundreds of PDF pages to fewer, organized disclosure records that are easy to review. For report authors, our tone framework gives feedback on whether their report mainly focuses on promises, actual operations, or results. For regulators and oversight groups, the design that keeps track of where information came from allows them to check disclosure claims without losing sight of the original page. For investors, the ratios related to greenwashing and the tone distributions can offer extra insights when going through many narrative disclosures.

More broadly, this thesis adds practical value by improving on current ESG automation in three key ways. First, it keeps a traceable record of information down to the page level. Second, it differentiates how mature disclosures are, instead of treating all positive ESG language as the same. Third, it shows where the system fails and where there's uncertainty, rather than hiding these behind a single overall score. These are important improvements for real-world application because making ESG decisions often relies as much on being able to trace information and understand it as it does on overall classification accuracy.

On a societal level, this work helps make sustainability communication more transparent. If used responsibly, a system like ours can help figure out when companies are mostly talking about aspirations, when they're reporting real actions, and when they're showing measurable results. This difference is important for holding companies accountable, for good sustainability governance, and for the public to better understand corporate claims about climate and ESG.

### 5.5.2 Ethical Risks, Bias, and Responsible Use

The main ethical concern is relying too much on automation. If people treat the extracted tone labels or greenwashing ratios as absolute truth instead of as tools to help make decisions, the system could misrepresent organizations or miss important details in legally or strategically sensitive disclosures. This risk is particularly high because our current evaluation system is still somewhat weakly supervised.

Concerns about bias and fairness also persist. Our current evidence tends to have more environmental and governance language and less from the social pillar. Also, the model and prompt outputs are affected by bilingual phrases, standard governance text, and layouts with many tables. Without careful checking, the system might consistently perform worse for certain industries, disclosure styles, or language patterns.

Privacy concerns are fairly minor because our collection of reports comes from publicly available corporate documents. However, responsible deployment still means being careful with how data is handled, cached, and how we manage origin logs and model outputs. Misuse is more likely to happen from misunderstanding the results than from exposing private data. For instance, a high greenwashing index should not be considered proof of wrongdoing without human review and outside confirmation.

Therefore, the necessary safeguards are clear: keep track of the source page, mark uncertainty and failures, require human review for important cases, increase annotations before using the system in high-stakes situations, and avoid using it as the only basis for regulatory or investment decisions. In terms of sustainability, this work fits with wider goals for responsible governance because it promotes a more transparent, evidence-based way of looking at ESG claims. The balanced view is that this is a useful technology for organizing and understanding ESG evidence, but its real value comes from being used carefully, with a focus on review, and with an understanding of the context.
