This paper argues that analyzing ESG disclosures in Indonesian sustainability reports gives us better information. It's not just about finding topics, but also about understanding the tone, knowing where the information came from, and linking it to a clear framework for extracting facts. What we found in Chapter 4 backs this up. Our process successfully took 23 sustainability reports, which were PDFs, and turned them into a searchable collection of about 5,512 pages. We ended up with 332 ESG records that showed the tone of the disclosure and 2,074 other data rows. We also connected 52 specific points to our framework. When we compared our results to ClimateBERT's commitment labels, we agreed 83.7% of the time, with a Cohen’s kappa score of 0.645.

Our most important finding is that the tone of a disclosure can be measured and it tells us something important. Most of the information we pulled out used a 'commitment' tone – 115 out of 332 records. Environmental disclosures were also the most common type, with 179 records. This shows that the company reports we looked at focus more on future promises and plans than on what they've actually achieved. This pattern is key because it proves why our research is needed: simply looking at whether something sounds 'positive' wouldn't tell you the difference between a real success and just a promise.

For this project, the best setup we found was using prompts that specifically asked for tone, combined with models that consistently followed our data structure. The arcee-ai/trinity-large-preview:free model worked especially well when given prompts designed for tone. This setup didn't just create more text; it made useful records with very few missing tones or structural errors, unlike other options. For example, openai/gpt-oss-120b:free could perfectly understand the text in a summary, but it completely missed the tone in every record and made structural errors 42.5% of the time in the same group. What this tells us is clear: for this task, making sure the data structure is followed and tone is captured is more important than how big or generally fluent a model is.

So, the most important technical takeaway from Chapter 4 isn't that one model was simply better. It's that how well the system works depends on how the prompts are designed and how the model responds to them. Our research had assumed that how sensitive the prompts were and how easily the structured output could break would be big factors in quality. Chapter 4 confirmed this. It also showed that our framework's ability to cover different topics (ontology) was quite strong, but figuring out the tone was the weakest part of the system. This both confirms and clarifies what we thought in Chapter 3. Chapter 3 expected some parts to be weak, and Chapter 4 shows that the main problem area is specifically with tone, not so much with scanning documents (OCR) or linking concepts to our framework.

Looking deeper, we found that different parts of our system need different kinds of information to work. The document scanning (OCR) part needs complete documents and to know exactly which page information comes from. The part that pulls out information relies on how we phrase the prompts, how well the model follows instructions, and how consistent its output structure is. The comparison part looks at how much our tone categories match climate topics. And the framework (ontology) part needs to cover all the relevant ESG words. Since these parts can fail for different reasons, it's good that we designed the system in separate modules.

The most important part of our whole process is how we designed the prompts to recognize tone. You can see this clearly in the prompt stability table: a standard prompt (data.md) could be processed, but it wasn't good at finding tone. On the other hand, prompts that used a 'chain-of-thought' approach for tone gave us many more records and missed less information. This tells us that the best results come from giving the model clear instructions to distinguish between promises, actions, and actual results, rather than just having a general ability to pull out text.

Different methods also use different kinds of information. Our rule-based model looks for specific words like 'berkomitmen' (commit), 'will,' 'target,' 'achieved,' and 'successfully.' The traditional TF-IDF model looks at how often words and characters appear. Our hybrid model is built to use information about the context and our framework. But the large language model (LLM) part relies on understanding the broader meaning and following instructions. This helps explain why some texts, especially those about governance or signing agreements, are tricky. They often talk about both actions and promises, so just looking for specific words or a general understanding can lead to different interpretations.

Research Question 3 asked how our tone-focused process compares to ClimateBERT's way of classifying climate-related information. The two are closely related, but they're not the same. Our agreement table shows that our 'commitment' tone labels matched ClimateBERT's climate-commitment labels 83.7% of the time, with a Cohen’s kappa score of 0.645 across 332 records. This is a strong finding because it shows our tone categories are useful, while also keeping them unique. In simple terms, ClimateBERT can tell us if a disclosure is about climate, but our tone labels can also tell us if it's a promise, an action, or something that's already happened.

Research Question 4 asked what problems our current evaluation and information extraction still have. The main weaknesses are missing tone information, errors in our data structure (schema drift), and text that is unclear or uses vague language. The strongest proof for this comes from the 61 times tone was completely missed, the 19 cases where the data structure drifted in predictions, problems with cautious or uncertain language, and how much results changed based on the prompt and model used. Our evaluation method is good enough to highlight these issues because it looks at everything: how well documents were scanned, if text was parsed correctly, how stable things were, what disagreements showed up, how well our framework covered topics, and early human-reviewed files. However, we can't claim our system is definitively superior yet because we still have limited expert-labeled data for comparison. Overall, answering these four questions shows that we've met the goal of this paper by creating an ESG disclosure analysis system that works, can be understood, and helps diagnose problems.

The first big limitation is that we don't have enough reference data. This paper doesn't use a complete set of expert-labeled examples for comparison yet. Instead, we used a mix of extracted data, comparisons to ClimateBERT labels, educated guesses, and early human-labeled files. This is fine for exploring and finding disagreements, but it means we can't make strong claims about how perfectly our model performs overall.

The second limitation is that our study focuses very specifically on certain areas and samples. Our results are based on Indonesian sustainability and annual reports, which often mix Indonesian and English. While this is good for understanding this specific area, it means our findings might not apply right away to other countries' rules, different types of documents, or reports in just one language. Also, there aren't many social pillar disclosures in the reports we looked at, so our findings about tone and aspects are more influenced by environmental and governance information.

The third limitation is how sensitive our models and prompts are. The prompt stability table shows that even prompts that are technically correct can still give us tone outputs that aren't useful. The model stability table shows that some models can perfectly understand the text but still mess up the meaning by missing tone or changing the data structure. This means our system isn't strong enough to just swap out its underlying technology. How well it works depends on carefully matching the prompt design with the model's behavior.

The fourth limitation is that there are technical failures that make it harder to understand the results. The table of failure modes lists 61 times tone was missed, 19 cases where the data structure drifted, problems with cautious language, issues with table layouts, and difficulties with specific Indonesian regulatory terms. These failures don't mean our system doesn't work, but they do mean that what we found in Chapter 4 should be seen as proof of a strong research system that still has its flaws.

The fifth limitation is that our greenwashing index is still just a rough guide. Ratios for companies like 73.0 for BEST-SR_2024 or 4.778 for vktr_ar_sr_2024 are interesting to look at, but we haven't checked them against official ESG ratings, actual legal cases, or opinions from human experts. So, right now, this index should only be used to help screen documents or start discussions, not as a final decision-making tool.

The quickest thing we can do to improve is to make our human annotation system bigger and more official. Our current early annotation files should be expanded into a structured, expert-reviewed standard where different annotators agree, especially for cases where it's hard to tell if something is a commitment or an action, for disclosures heavy on governance, and for records currently flagged as needing review. This would directly fix the weakest part of how we evaluate the system.

The second quick improvement is to make our tone-specific prompt engineering and data structure enforcement stronger. Our results already show that prompts using a 'chain-of-thought' approach for tone work better than general extraction prompts. The next step is to turn this into a more organized prompt process, which would include stricter checks on the output, confidence scores for each data field, and a way to re-run the process specifically for records where tone was missed.

The third quick improvement is to check the quality of our document scanning (OCR). Our data currently shows that OCR scanning works, but it doesn't yet report how many character errors there are, how accurate table extraction is, or the basic quality of meaning on each page. Adding these checks would help us figure out if problems are coming from the initial scanning or from the tone classification later on.

The fourth quick improvement is to do a direct comparison with ClimateBERT across all the records we extracted. Our data already allows us to create batches of records for this. Finishing that comparison would turn our current rough comparison into a much stronger external reference point.

The fifth quick improvement is to do a better job of filtering out text that isn't about ESG or accounting policies. Our early human-labeled files show cases where financial statements were correctly ignored, but if we filtered these out more clearly at the beginning, we'd waste less processing power and get better results later.

Longer term, this research could become a bigger project focusing on using computers to analyze sustainability disclosures in places with limited resources or where multiple languages are used. One path is to train our models more: we could improve our hybrid system by fine-tuning it with a larger set of Indonesian ESG tone data. This would let the system learn how to tell different tones apart more directly, instead of relying so much on just the prompts.

Another path is to make the system more robust and able to work in different areas. We should test our framework on reports that are longer, messier, and from more varied industries. Then, we should try it in other countries' regulatory settings, beyond just Indonesia. This would show if our tone categories and framework structure are still useful in different reporting cultures.

A third path is research focused on putting the system into use. Since our data already keeps track of where information came from, down to the page, and includes many useful tools, we could turn it into a review system for analysts – for regulators, ESG research teams, investors, or auditors. In that case, the main research question would change from 'Can the model categorize things?' to 'How should people and models work together when the quality of the information isn't clear?'

A fourth path involves research on graphs and combining knowledge. Our framework and how it exports meaning already suggest we could use graph-based searches and similar methods. Future work could look at how organized ESG information, the links within our framework, and knowing the original page can help us find information in a way that can be easily traced back, instead of just creating static reports.

All these future ideas form a clear plan: first, make our annotation and comparison methods more stable; then, make our models stronger and improve document scanning quality; next, use the system in more areas and for practical uses; and finally, weave this whole process into a bigger system for evidence-based research.

This research will directly help ESG analysts, people who write sustainability reports, regulators, investors, and research teams focused on how companies are held accountable. For ESG analysts, our system makes it cheaper to go from hundreds of PDF pages to fewer, organized, and reviewable records of disclosures. For those writing sustainability reports, our tone framework can tell them if their report mostly talks about promises, actions, or actual results. For regulators and oversight groups, the system that keeps track of where information came from means they can check disclosure claims without losing the link to the original page. For investors, the greenwashing-related numbers and tone patterns can offer extra clues when they read many narrative reports.

More broadly, this paper's practical value is that it makes current ESG automation better in three ways. First, it keeps a clear, auditable record of where information came from, down to the page. Second, it tells the difference in how mature a disclosure is, instead of treating all positive ESG language as the same. Third, it shows where the system fails and where there's uncertainty, rather than hiding it behind one overall score. These are important improvements for real-world use because making decisions about ESG often needs to know where information came from and how to understand it, just as much as it needs accurate categorization.

For society, this work helps make information about sustainability more open and clear. If used carefully, a system like this can help us see when companies are mostly talking about their hopes, when they're reporting real actions, and when they're showing measurable results. This difference is important for holding companies accountable, managing sustainability, and helping the public understand what companies claim about climate and ESG.

The main ethical concern is relying too much on automation. If people take the extracted tone labels or greenwashing scores as absolute truth instead of just tools to help make decisions, the system could wrongly describe organizations or hide important details in legally or strategically sensitive disclosures. This risk is particularly high because our current evaluation system is still partly supervised without full human oversight.

Concerns about bias and fairness also remain. The information we're currently using focuses too much on environmental and governance language, and not enough on the social pillar. Also, what the model and prompts produce is sensitive to mixed-language phrasing, standard governance text, and reports with many tables. Without careful checking, the system might consistently do poorly for certain industries, ways of disclosing information, or language styles.

Privacy concerns are fairly small because we use public company reports. However, using the system responsibly still means being careful with how we handle data, store temporary information (caching), keep track of where data came from, and manage the model's outputs. Problems are more likely to come from misinterpreting the results than from exposing private data. For instance, a high greenwashing index shouldn't be seen as proof of wrongdoing without human review and checking with other sources.

So, the necessary safeguards are clear: always be able to trace information back to its original page, keep uncertainty and failure warnings visible, require human review for important cases, add more human annotations before using the system for critical tasks, and don't use the system as the only basis for legal or investment decisions. In terms of sustainability, this work fits with the larger goals of responsible governance because it promotes a more open and evidence-based way of reading ESG claims. The balanced view is that this technology is helpful for organizing and understanding ESG information, but its usefulness depends on being used carefully, with review, and with an understanding of the situation.
