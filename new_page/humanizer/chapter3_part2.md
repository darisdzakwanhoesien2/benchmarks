The proposed system is not just one simple classifier. It’s a complete method built around a structured storage for ESG evidence. The features we pulled out are used in two main ways: first, to create structured records from report text; and second, to check and improve those records by comparing them, benchmarking them, and analyzing them with our ontology.

The first part of our system is an LLM workflow that looks at each page. It mainly runs through 'pages/llm_processing.py' and 'code/llm_background_worker.py'. Its goal is to turn report text, which has been expanded by OCR, into organized ESG records. These records have fields like text section, aspect, pillar, tone, sentiment, and the reasoning behind them. The system takes documents or batches of pages, adds prompt templates, sends these requests to specific model servers, and then saves the standardized results in 'results/esg_records.json'.

We have many different prompt types, including zero-shot, few-shot, chain-of-thought, and ones focused on tone. These prompts are available in both Indonesian and English. We also have data-specific prompt files like 'data.md' and 'data_v1.md'. This variety in prompts helps us check if the quality of the output changes based on how we structure the information, the language used, or the way the model reasons.

Simply put, the system takes a group of page texts (B) with a specific prompt template (q) and model (m). It then produces a structured set of results (R). This can be written as R = F(B, q, m), where F is the full extraction process. We only accept R if it can be correctly read into our expected record format. If not, we keep the run with error details so we can check it later, instead of just deleting it without notice.

The second part of the system handles validation and comparison. It uses outputs from T1 and T2, comparisons similar to ClimateBERT, and checks that align with our ontology. The file 'pages/ground_truth.py' loads the texts that were extracted from 'results/esg_records.json', creates text units for benchmarking, and saves the results in 'results/t1_results.jsonl' and 'results/t2_results.jsonl' so we can pick up where we left off. T1 mainly deals with outputs like those from ClimateBERT or other local classification models. T2, on the other hand, collects labels based on rules or a mix of rules and aspect-based sentiment analysis, along with their related processes.

We use JSONL format because it lets us add each processed item separately. This also means we can easily restart the process and skip items that are already done. Functions like 'load_processed_t1()' and 'load_processed_t2()' use consistent keys, like pairs of labels and models, or just labels, to prevent re-processing items that are finished. This is important for our method because creating benchmarks can be paused by issues like models not being available, jobs taking too long, or part of the process failing. Our study needs a reliable way to pick up exactly where it left off.

Later, we combine evidence from various sources. The system compares the tone labels we extracted with labels from methods like ClimateBERT. It also connects the extracted aspects to their relevant categories in our ontology. We measure how often tone is missing and if the data structure changes. Any disagreements are saved for someone to review manually. So, our scoring isn't based on just one number; instead, it's a set of indicators for evidence quality, including how well data is parsed, how complete the fields are, if labels agree, how much our ontology covers, and what types of failures occur.

In this system, "inference" is more than just creating a label. It means turning the model's results into useful parts of our research. For T3 extraction, we convert the raw output into structured records. For T1 and T2 benchmarking, we link the JSONL outputs back to their labels and record texts. When analyzing the ontology, we connect the extracted aspects to their official paths. For checking where information came from, we link statements back to their original page markdown. And for dashboard reports, we put all these results together into CSV summaries, graphs, and tables that tell the story of the evidence.

This mapping step is very important. Our goal is to create disclosure evidence that can actually be used, not just isolated classification scores. A tone label that the model predicts isn't very helpful if we can't link it back to a specific record, its original page, its place in the ontology, and how it compares to other data. Because of this, our system considers output mapping a core part of its method.

We don't have a complete set of data that has been fully labeled by experts yet. This is a major challenge for our study, and we designed our system to work with this limitation, not against it. So, our process uses several types of reference data: basic labels from what the system extracts, benchmark results in JSONL format, small sets of human-labeled data, and comparison labels from ClimateBERT-like runs.

The current outputs aren't good enough to be our final, perfect labels. The records we extract come from prompts and models, which means their structure can change, they might miss fields, or they might give different results with different templates. Labels from ClimateBERT-style models are helpful for comparing externally, but they don't fully get the tone of the disclosures. We only have a small amount of human-labeled data. Because of this, our study needs a specific way to build our reference data that combines automated steps with some manual checking.

A quick look at the dashboard shows this problem clearly. It indicates 70 pilot labels and points out that we need more robust human-coded baselines, examples of disagreements, and wider evaluation. So, our method sees building reference data as a step-by-step process: initial, less certain labels help with early tests, pilot labels help with specific checks, and future work can aim for a more solid, categorized set of perfect labels.

We start creating reference data from the records extracted and saved in 'results/esg_records.json'. The script 'pages/ground_truth.py' takes the main text fields, any text within nested records, and other raw outputs that can be parsed, and turns them into text units for benchmarking. These units then go through the T1 and T2 pipelines, and their results are added line by line to 'results/t1_results.jsonl' and 'results/t2_results.jsonl'. Using JSONL means we can easily restart the process and check everything later.

For T1, the system can use a ClimateBERT client if one is set up, or it can use other local models found in its cache. For T2, we use rules and a mix of logic to get outputs related to tone, sentiment, and our ontology. Also, 'pages/1_4_ClimateBERT_Record_Batch.py' prepares batches of records, one by one, for external checking with ClimateBERT. It specifically keeps the 'record_id' so that when results come back, they can be matched to the correct original records.

Humans get involved in the review process using pilot annotation files and Streamlit pages designed for reviewing. 'results/revision_analysis/pilot_ground_truth_seed.csv' sets up the structure for annotations, and 'pilot_ground_truth_annotations.csv' holds the labels that have been reviewed. These files don't make up a full, perfect dataset yet, but they're enough to allow us to check specific parts, do early agreement tests, and find challenging examples.

We check the generated reference data in two ways: structurally and semantically. Structurally, we only consider a record complete if it has all the expected labels and no clear errors. Semantically, our agreement pages and review queues look at whether the tone, sentiment, and ClimateBERT-style labels make sense together or if they contradict each other. Pages that show the origin of the data also help by confirming that the extracted statements can be found in the original OCR text.

Still, our current reference data has some important limits. The basic labels rely on the models used. How we phrase the prompts affects which records are even extracted. We keep an eye on OCR quality, but we haven't fully measured it yet with formal character error rates (CER) or word error rates (WER) for the data we're actively using. While our ontology covers a lot in the current dashboard view, its reach still depends on what our existing ontology resources include. Because of all this, this study doesn't say it has a final, perfect benchmark. Instead, it claims to have a process for building reference data that can be checked and expanded, which is good for ongoing thesis research.

The way our research area is always changing limits how internally valid our work is. We developed and refined prompts, compared models, expanded the ontology, and created review tools all in the same project, step by step. This makes the engineering process more transparent, but it also means you shouldn't see this study as a completely fixed benchmark where development and evaluation were kept entirely separate from the start.

Our construct validity is limited because things like disclosure tone, sentiment, climate topic labels, and greenwashing scores are connected but not the same. To deal with this, the study analyzes these parts separately, though it can't completely remove all the conceptual overlap. So, we treat outputs from ClimateBERT-style models about commitment as an external way to compare meaning, not as the absolute truth for tone.

The external validity is limited by focusing on one specific area. The data we use comes from Indonesian sustainability and annual reports. These often have mixed Indonesian and English phrasing and follow specific reporting rules for their industries. This means our findings apply more strongly to this specific setting than to other regulatory, linguistic, or industry environments.

Our work is very reproducible when it comes to the saved files, prompts, logs, and outputs that let us resume work. However, it's less reproducible if you expect the exact same results from re-running third-party LLMs. Things like whether a model is available, updates from the model provider, and the random nature of generation can change future outputs, even if we keep the prompt files and artifact locations. Because of this, the study is more confident about having a reproducible workflow structure and reproducible evidence storage than about getting perfectly identical results every time in the future.

This chapter outlined a practical method for analyzing Indonesian ESG disclosures. The process starts with PDF reports, turns them into page data using OCR, and then converts that data into structured ESG records using different LLM prompts. We then make these records richer using rules, traditional machine learning, and mixed contextual information. After that, we connect them to our ontology categories and comparison labels like those from ClimateBERT. Finally, we save the entire process in reproducible result stores and dashboard items.

From a methods point of view, this design is valuable not just because it automates extraction. It also does so in a way that allows us to check everything, change parts easily, handle both Indonesian and English, and work with incomplete reference data. This means the next chapter can focus on how we actually built and got our results, using a clear data flow, model structure, and evaluation reference layer that is based on the code and files in our project.
