Introduction

The growing prominence of ESG and climate-related disclosures in corporate reporting underscores their relevance for both scholarly insight and decision-making in markets, policy, and practice. Academically, disclosures anchor analyses of corporate sustainability performance, governance, and risk management, enabling empirical work on how firms integrate environmental and social considerations into strategy, capital allocation, and accountability mechanisms Ni et al., 2023; Jin, 2025; (Hang, 2025; . For decision-makers, disclosures inform investment screening, regulatory compliance, and stakeholder governance, providing the evidentiary basis for assessing risk exposure, resilience to climate transition, and alignment with normative frameworks such as international reporting standards and disclosure mandates (Hang, 2025; (Khak et al., 2025; Kaoukis, 2025; . Yet the very purpose of these disclosures—communication to heterogeneous audiences under evolving regulatory regimes—generates data that are not trivially usable as machine-readable signals. Studies across domains emphasize that even structured or semi-structured text in sustainability reports contains nuanced context, provenance, and interpretive layers that complicate automated analysis and cross-company comparability (Sintas, 2020; Kirby, 2023; (Khak et al., 2025; . A synthesis of recent work shows that robust evidence extraction, benchmarking, and surveillance require careful attention to data lineage, standardization gaps, and the heterogeneity of sources, formats, and languages (Khak et al., 2025; Leal, 2023; Jin, 2025; .

Heterogeneity remains a central obstacle to analytics even in the absence of OCR. ESG and climate disclosures are frequently produced as PDFs that embed complex layout patterns, tables, footnotes, and boilerplate language, often interleaved with narrative sections that carry materiality signals and temporal targets. The reading order and structural cues embedded in these documents are highly variable across issuers, markets, and reporting frameworks, which undermines naive text extraction and simple token-based parsing (Khak et al., 2025; Li, 2025; . In addition, the material content is distributed across multilingual contexts, with jurisdiction-specific terminology and standards adding another layer of complexity for uniform analytics. Prior work in document understanding and ESG parsing highlights layout-aware approaches that preserve section hierarchies, table structures, and figure captions to maintain semantic units essential for reliable extraction and comparability (Mishra et al., 2024; Chen, 2025; Zhao et al., 2025). Together, these factors imply that effective ESG/climate analytics demand architecture that respects document morphology, provenance, and multilinguality even when OCR is not deployed.

LLMs offer a path forward by providing flexible, context-aware extraction, normalization, and reasoning over diverse textual forms. When integrated into calibrated pipelines, LLMs can map heterogeneous narratives to standardized representations, infer implicit relations (e.g., targets, baselines, and time horizons), and synthesize evidence across multiple passages. This capability supports scalable discovery, cross-document reconciliation, and intelligible reporting for analysts and decision-makers (Khak et al., 2025; (Hang, 2025; Kaoukis, 2025; . However, deploying LLMs introduces new risks. Hallucinations and misalignment with reporting standards can generate misleading impressions of a company’s climate commitments or ESG performance, particularly in cases of sparse or ambiguous passages. Prompt sensitivity and model drift can lead to inconsistent extractions across time or between similar documents, raising concerns about reliability and auditability. Consequently, reliability considerations must accompany any LLM-enabled workflow, with emphasis on traceability to source passages, confidence estimates, and human-in-the-loop safeguards where appropriate (Khak et al., 2025; Ni et al., 2023; Zitu et al., 2025; Schmoll, 2025).

Scope and contributions of this work are defined with a clear boundary: this Introduction lays the groundwork for a digital-text–focused review of LLM-assisted ESG/climate-disclosure analytics pipelines, explicitly excluding OCR-centric processing from the core scope. The subsequent sections will articulate a layout-aware, chunking-guided pipeline design, retrieval-augmented extraction, cross-document evaluation, and governance practices suitable for near-term deployment without relying on scanned-image to text conversion. Our explicit contributions are: (i) a formal problem framing that separates data-quality, structure, and provenance from raw textual content; (ii) a taxonomy of core pipeline components tailored to digital-text ESG/climate disclosures, including layout-aware parsing, semantic chunking, retrieval, LLM-based extraction and normalization, and evaluation; (iii) a synthesis of best-practice recommendations for data architecture, tooling, evaluation protocols, and governance controls grounded in empirical and system-as-a-whole perspectives; and (iv) practical guidance for researchers and practitioners on reliability-sensitive deployment, including transparent reporting of confidence, limitations, and failure modes. We illustrate the framework with a running example that demonstrates how an emissions target and its corroborating evidence can be extracted and traced through the pipeline, highlighting both capabilities and caveats in a real-world scenario.

Running example: Suppose a company discloses an annual target to reduce Scope 1 and 2 greenhouse gas emissions to achieve a 30% reduction by 2030, with a tabular baseline year, a progress metric in metric tons CO2e, and a referenced external standard. The pipeline identifies the target sentence in the narrative, locates the corresponding table row and the baseline year, retrieves corroborating passages (e.g., prior-year disclosures and external standard references), and outputs a structured record: {company, target: “Scope 1+2 emissions reduction 30% by 2030”, baseline: 2024, metric: "tCO2e", baseline-value, target-year: 2030, evidence: passages source passages, provenance: page/section, confidence: high/medium/low}. This example will be used to illustrate extraction semantics, provenance tracing, and evaluation considerations throughout the paper.
