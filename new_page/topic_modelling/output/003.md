Section 2. Background: What Topic Modelling Is (and Isn’t)

This section defines topic modelling in both bag-of-words and embedding-based paradigms, and discusses why topics can be misleading in ESG disclosure corpora. It also clarifies the unit of analysis and the trade-offs involved, finishing with a recommendation to use both statement-level and OCR-chunk representations to support robust ESG insights.

2.1 What constitutes a “topic”? Bag-of-words versus embedding-based paradigms

Bag-of-words (BoW) topic models: In classic BoW approaches (e.g., Latent Dirichlet Allocation, LDA), a topic is a probability distribution over a finite vocabulary. Documents are modeled as mixtures of topics, and each topic is characterized by a word distribution that reflects co-occurring terms, typically captured through word co-occurrence patterns (P(topic | document)) and (P(word | topic)) (Strien et al., 2020; , Zosa et al., 2021; , Mutuvi et al., 2018; .
Embedding-based topic models: In embedding-based paradigms, a topic is represented as a cluster or a dense region in a learned semantic space rather than as a fixed word-list. Topics emerge from document or token embeddings (e.g., using transformer-derived vectors) and a clustering or density-based partitioning (e.g., BERTopic, ETM). A topic is thus a semantically coherent set of high-density representations, with representative terms derived from nearest-neighbor or salience-weighted words in the embedding space, and topic distributions over documents inferred from the cluster assignment or proximity to topic centroids Zosa et al., 2021; , Mutuvi et al., 2018; , Hämäläinen & Hengchen, 2019).
Cross-paradigm synthesis: Across both paradigms, topics aim to summarize themes that recur across documents. The BoW view emphasizes explicit word co-occurrence patterns, while embedding-based methods emphasize semantic proximity and contextualized usage, which can yield more interpretable topics in noisy or multilingual data when tuned appropriately Zosa et al., 2021; , Mutuvi et al., 2018; , Hämäläinen & Hengchen, 2019).
2.2 Why “topics” can be misleading in ESG disclosure corpora

2.2.1 Junk topic sources in ESG corpora (4–6 bullets)

Boilerplate content and standard sections: ESG reports contain repetitive boilerplate language (e.g., governance statements, risk disclosures, disclaimers) that can dominate topics and obscure material sustainability issues (Strien et al., 2020; , Hamdi et al., 2022; , Hämäläinen & Hengchen, 2019).
Section headings and document structure: Explicit headings (e.g., “Environmental Policy,” “Supplier Code of Conduct”) introduce artificial topical signals that may be more about document structure than substantive themes, biasing topic discovery (Strien et al., 2020; , Hamdi et al., 2022; , Hämäläinen & Hengchen, 2019).
OCR artifacts and noise: Character errors, line breaks, and segmentation issues from OCR produce spurious tokens or broken phrases that cluster into meaningless topics or degrade coherence, especially in long, dense ESG narratives (Strien et al., 2020; , Hamdi et al., 2022; , Mutuvi et al., 2018; , Hämäläinen & Hengchen, 2019).
Proper nouns and organization names: Repeated company names, partner institutions, or regulatory bodies can create dominant, non-informative topics if not properly disambiguated or normalized, leading to topic drift across documents (Strien et al., 2020; , Hamdi et al., 2022; , Hämäläinen & Hengchen, 2019).
Citations and figures captions: Frequent inline citations or figure/table captions may form topical pockets that reflect formatting rather than substantive sustainability discourse, potentially biasing topic models if not filtered (Strien et al., 2020; , Hamdi et al., 2022; , Hämäläinen & Hengchen, 2019).
Multilingual and code-switch terms: Indonesian-English mixing and domain-specific jargon can create fragmented topics that are difficult to interpret when language cues are not aligned with the model’s vocabulary or embeddings Zosa et al., 2021; , Mutuvi et al., 2018; , Hämäläinen & Hengchen, 2019).
2.2.2 Why external validation is needed (pillars/aspects)

ESG taxonomy alignment: Topics should map to established ESG dimensions (Environmental, Social, Governance) or to issuer-specific materiality frameworks to ensure interpretability and comparability across firms (Strien et al., 2020; , Hamdi et al., 2022; , Hämäläinen & Hengchen, 2019).
ABSA and sentiment relevance: Beyond thematic coherence, topics should cohere with aspect-based sentiment cues (e.g., environmental impact, governance controls, social programs) to provide actionable insights for stakeholders, regulators, and investors (Strien et al., 2020; , Hamdi et al., 2022; , Hämäläinen & Hengchen, 2019).
Materiality and stakeholder relevance: External validation requires cross-walking topics to material sustainability issues and stakeholder concerns, ensuring that discovered themes reflect real economic, environmental, or social materiality rather than stylistic artifacts (Strien et al., 2020; , Hamdi et al., 2022; , Hämäläinen & Hengchen, 2019).
Cross-corpus stability and reproducibility: Validation across time (years), companies, and document batches tests whether topics capture stable, policy-relevant themes rather than idiosyncratic noise or boilerplate recurrences (Strien et al., 2020; , Hamdi et al., 2022; , Hämäläinen & Hengchen, 2019).
OCR-robust baselines: Given OCR noise, external validation should benchmark topic usefulness against OCR-cleaned or post-corrected baselines, ensuring that improvements reflect genuine semantic signals rather than formatting artifacts or tokenization quirks (Strien et al., 2020; , Hamdi et al., 2022; , Mutuvi et al., 2018; , Hämäläinen & Hengchen, 2019).
2.3 Unit of analysis: the defining choice

Report level
Advantages: Captures broad, system-wide themes across entire disclosures; simple to implement; reduced fragmentation.
Disadvantages: Low granularity; risk of topic dilution; harder to localize insights to specific governance issues or environmental programs; sensitive to document-length variability (Strien et al., 2020; , Moreno & Caminero, 2020; , Aryoseto et al., 2023).
Section level
Advantages: Aligns with the document’s logical structure; improves interpretability by linking topics to governance sections or environmental programs; better granularity than report-level.
Disadvantages: Requires reliable section segmentation; still vulnerable to boilerplate clustering within sections; moderate noise from cross-sectional language (Strien et al., 2020; , Moreno & Caminero, 2020; , Aryoseto et al., 2023).
Page or chunk level (OCR-derived chunks)
Advantages: Balances granularity and scalability; can capture topic evolution across sections or pages; suitable for long documents with clear sectional breaks.
Disadvantages: OCR chunking may introduce boundary artifacts; varying chunk sizes complicate comparisons; noise sensitivity is higher due to shorter text spans (Strien et al., 2020; , Mutuvi et al., 2018; , Hämäläinen & Hengchen, 2019).
Statement level
Advantages: Highest granularity; precise alignment with specific claims, metrics, or disclosures; supports fine-grained ABSA mapping.
Disadvantages: Extremely sparse signals per unit; high noise risk; computationally intensive; interpretation can be brittle if statements are fragmented by OCR (Strien et al., 2020; , Mutuvi et al., 2018; , Hämäläinen & Hengchen, 2019).
Trade-offs overview
Topic granularity: Finer units (statements, chunks) enable targeted insights but demand more robust noise handling; coarser units (report, sections) improve stability but reduce actionable precision.
Noise sensitivity: Shorter units magnify OCR-induced tokenization errors and semantic drift; longer units dilute noisy signals but may conflate distinct topics.
Interpretability: User-facing interpretability benefits from units that map cleanly to governance or ESG taxonomy constructs; over-fragmentation may hinder stakeholders’ ability to relate topics to concrete disclosures (Strien et al., 2020; , Hamdi et al., 2022; , Hämäläinen & Hengchen, 2019).
2.4 Recommendation

Practical guidance: Given the mixed quality and structure of Indonesian bilingual ESG disclosures, a dual-unit strategy is recommended. Employ statement-level modelling to capture precise, actionable ESG claims and to support ABSA-aligned interpretation. Concurrently, construct an OCR-chunk (e.g., section- or page-derived) corpus to provide a more stable, project-level view that mitigates the fragility of sentence- or statement-level signals. This combination leverages the high-resolution fidelity of statements for fine-grained insights while benefiting from the robustness and comparability afforded by larger, chunk-based aggregates. Such a hybrid approach aligns with evidence on OCR robustness, topic coherence, and the value of multi-scale analysis reported across BoW and embedding-based topic modelling literature, particularly in noisy, multilingual ESG contexts (Strien et al., 2020; , Zosa et al., 2021; , Hamdi et al., 2022; , Mutuvi et al., 2018; , Hämäläinen & Hengchen, 2019).
