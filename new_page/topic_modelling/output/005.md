Section 4. Preprocessing for OCR and Bilingual Sustainability Reports

This section outlines practical preprocessing steps tailored to OCR-derived, bilingual Indonesian ESG disclosures. It covers OCR-specific cleaning, boilerplate handling, bilingual tokenization and normalization, and the definition of canonical metadata to support reproducible, evaluation-friendly pipelines. The recommendations emphasize how each preprocessing choice influences topic quality, interpretability, and robustness in long, multilingual ESG corpora.

4.1 OCR-specific cleaning

Core idea:
OCR noise can severely distort tokenization and semantic signals; targeted cleaning mitigates errors that would otherwise corrupt topic discovery.

Recommended steps (4–7 concrete steps):

1) De-noise character-level errors via lightweight post-correction pass

What to do: Apply a rule-based or lightweight statistical correction for obvious mis-OCR’d characters (e.g., common confusions like l/1, O/0, S/5) within a domain-relevant lexicon.
Why it matters: Reduces token fragmentation and spurious vocabulary, improving token co-occurrence patterns and topic coherence.
2) Correct line breaks and hyphenation artifacts

What to do: Normalize broken words at line endings and remove stray hyphens that split words across line breaks.
Why it matters: Restores lexical integrity, enabling more stable word distributions and reducing topic fragmentation caused by artificial word breaks.
3) Normalize punctuation and stray diacritics

What to do: Remove or standardize unusual punctuation and diacritics introduced by OCR when they do not reflect Indonesian or English orthography; collapse repeated punctuation.
Why it matters: Improves tokenization stability and reduces noisy token sequences that spawn irrelevant topics.
4) Layout-aware segmentation correction

What to do: Use document structure cues (page numbers, columns, marginalia) to correct mis-split sentences or mis-scanned sections; optionally resegment with a simple, layout-aware splitter.
Why it matters: Improves the alignment between textual units and topic units, supporting more coherent topic discovery across sections.
5) Filter non-textual or low-quality pages

What to do: Remove pages that are largely figures, tables, or scanned as low-resolution images; optionally flag and separate them for OCR reprocessing.
Why it matters: Focuses modelling on high-signal text, reducing noise from non-informative regions that can distort topic signals.
6) Language detection at segment level

What to do: Run lightweight language detection to tag Indonesian vs. English segments; keep language tags for downstream multilingual processing.
Why it matters: Enables language-aware embeddings and better cross-language topic alignment; prevents mixing language-specific noise into shared topics.
7) Token-level normalization with domain-aware lexicon

What to do: Normalize numerals, units, and company names consistently; optionally apply simple stemming or lemmatization compatible with Indonesian and English.
Why it matters: Encourages stable term representations across documents, aiding cross-document topic coherence.
4.2 Boilerplate and template language handling

Strategies (3–6 items):

1) Boilerplate deduplication

What to do: Identify high-frequency boilerplate blocks (standard governance statements, disclaimers) and remove or extract them before modelling.
Why it matters: Prevents boilerplate from dominating topics, leading to more meaningful discovery of sustainability-related themes.
2) Stoplists tuned to ESG domains (and bilingual)

What to do: Develop domain-specific stopword lists that cover common ESG boilerplate, repeated section headings, and transitional phrases specific to Indonesian and English.
Why it matters: Reduces noise from non-informative common text and helps topics focus on substantive disclosures.
3) Downweight or mask boilerplate regions

What to do: Assign lower weights to tokens from boilerplate sections or apply a section-aware masking during feature construction (e.g., TF-IDF downweighting or topic model priors).
Why it matters: Shifts topic signal toward material ESG content rather than repetitive templates.
4) Structural chunking and segmentation

What to do: Segment documents into meaningful structural units (sections, subsections, or paragraphs) and treat boilerplate blocks as separate units or exclude them from topic inference.
Why it matters: Improves topic interpretability by aligning topics with governance areas rather than document scaffolding.
5) Deduplication across documents

What to do: Detect and collapse near-duplicate boilerplate passages across reports to avoid topic overweighting influenced by re-used templates.
Why it matters: Enhances cross-document comparability and topic stability.
6) Validation against a boilerplate-annotated corpus

What to do: Periodically evaluate the extent of boilerplate leakage into topics and adjust cleaning strategies accordingly.
Why it matters: Keeps the pipeline adaptable to new reporting templates and regulatory changes.
Boilerplate distorts topics and evaluation:
Boilerplate phrases inflate topic frequency and cause topics to reflect formatting rather than substantive ESG content. This distorts coherence (topics cluster around generic phrases), reduces interpretability (topics no longer map cleanly to ESG dimensions), and undermines extrinsic evaluation against ESG taxonomies or ABSA cues. Effective boilerplate handling improves both intrinsic coherence and extrinsic validity by ensuring that topics reflect material environmental, social, and governance disclosures rather than template padding.

4.3 Bilingual tokenization and normalization

Practical guidance:

Stopwords:

Build and use a bilingual stopword list that covers Indonesian and English stopwords, as well as common ESG boilerplate terms.
Consider language-aware stopword application so that tokens are not prematurely removed in code-switched segments where content-bearing words may appear in both languages.
Light normalization:

Apply language-aware lowercasing, diacritic normalization, and whitespace normalization. Preserve proper nouns and company names; avoid aggressive stemming that could break cross-language alignment.
Normalize numbers, units, and abbreviations consistently across languages to support comparability.
Multilingual embeddings:

Use multilingual or cross-lingual embeddings (e.g., multilingual BERT, XLM-R, or language-adapted Indonesian-English encoders) to enable coherent cross-language topic representations.
Align vocabulary to the domain with domain-specific tunings or adapters if available; consider embedding-space post-processing (orthogonalization, alignment) to improve cross-language coherence.
Risk of “language topics” and mitigations:

Danger: The model may produce topics dominated by language-specific tokens (e.g., Indonesian-only tokens vs. English-only tokens), which can appear as separate “language topics” rather than thematic topics.
Mitigations:
Use language tags or language-aware embeddings to encourage cross-language mixing in the same topic when semantically appropriate.
Employ cross-lingual alignment objectives or bilingual priors in probabilistic models or cluster centroids in embedding-based methods.
Evaluate topics for cross-language semantic coherence, not merely linguistic homogeneity; perform manual or semi-automatic ABSA-aligned labeling to ensure thematic relevance across languages.
4.4 Defining canonical metadata

Metadata fields (recommended):

Company: Name of the issuer or reporting entity (normalized to a canonical form).
Year: Publication year of the ESG report (or the fiscal year covered).
Sector: Industry classification (e.g., GICS/IFRS sector code or locally relevant taxonomy).
Source_type: Indicates the document kind (e.g., sustainability report, integrated report, CSR report, annual report).
Language tags: Language labels per document or per segment (e.g., id, en, id-en).
Edition/Version: If multiple revisions or re-scans exist for the same document.
OCRqualitytag: A coarse or precise indicator of OCR quality (e.g., high, medium, low, or a numeric score).
Section or chunk identifiers: Optional per-unit identifiers that link to the corresponding structural segments (e.g., Section 3.1 Environmental Policy).
Table-like bullet list (canonical metadata):

Company: string
Year: YYYY
Sector: string or code
Source_type: sustainability report / integrated report / CSR / annual report
Language: id / en / mixed
Edition/Version: optional string
OCRqualitytag: e.g., high, medium, low; or a numeric score
Segment_id: optional per-unit identifier linking to sections or chunks
Section_path: optional hierarchical path, e.g., "Environmental > Emissions > Scope 1"
Versioning corpora for reproducibility:

Manifest: Maintain a manifest file (e.g., JSON or YAML) listing every corpus item with fields: documentid, company, year, sourcetype, language, edition, sourcepath, totaltokens, OCRqualitytag, and a checksum (e.g., SHA-256) of the raw OCR text.
Corpus signature: Compute a corpus-level signature (e.g., aggregated token histograms, language distribution, and a hash of preprocessing parameters) to detect drift between runs.
Version control strategy: Treat the raw OCR-derived text, preprocessing scripts, and metadata as versioned artifacts. Use a data versioning system (e.g., DVC, Quilt, or Git-LFS) to track changes and enable reproducible experiments.
Reproducibility workflow: Before each experiment, snapshot the exact manifest and corpus signature; store the code, preprocessing configuration, and evaluation metrics alongside the data version to enable exact replication of results.
Notes:

The metadata should be designed to support cross-year and cross-company comparisons, enabling robust longitudinal ESG analyses.
The manifest and signature approach supports traceability, auditability, and the ability to reproduce results even as reporting formats or OCR quality evolve over time.
